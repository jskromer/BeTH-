## A. Campaign site (kromerforberkeley.org/budget) — insert above or beside the department table

### The budget is $905 million. The deficit lives in one third of it.

Berkeley's FY2027 budget is **$905 million** across all funds. About **$315 million** of that is the **General Fund** — the part the Council can spend on anything. The other $590 million is already spoken for: voter-approved parcel taxes that can only go to parks, libraries, fire, or streets; water, sewer, marina and parking fees that must stay in the operation that collected them; state and federal grants with strings attached; bond money committed to specific projects.

When you read that Berkeley has a **$32 million structural deficit**, that is a General Fund number. When the Council cuts positions, moves programs to other funds, or asks for a half-cent sales tax, it is the General Fund it is balancing. The other two thirds of the budget can be large, growing, and irrelevant to the deficit at the same time.

**Where the $315 million comes from.** Property tax is the base: about $99 million from the general levy, plus $21 million in property-transfer tax and another $12 million from Measure P's higher transfer-tax rate on expensive sales. Business-license tax brings $25 million, sales tax $19 million, utility-users tax $19 million, the state's vehicle-license backfill $21 million, interest on the City's cash $14 million. Roughly five dollars in six are taxes; the rest is fees, fines, and reimbursements from other funds.

**Where it goes.** Two thirds of General Fund spending is salaries and benefits. Public safety — police, fire, 911 — is a little over half of the total (FY2025 audited: $147 million of $261 million). Only about four in ten City employees are paid from the General Fund, and most of those are police and fire. That is why every General Fund balancing plan ends up at the same three doors: public-safety staffing, pension and health-benefit costs, and the small set of programs the General Fund pays for directly.

**How FY2027 was balanced.** The City projected $297 million in revenue against $329 million in spending — a $32 million gap. It closed the gap by re-basing the personnel budget to what departments actually spend ($16.5 million), a position-reduction list of 138 positions (100 of them vacant), and revised revenue forecasts. The adopted budget shows a $1 million surplus. Thirty-three of the preserved positions are funded only if the November sales-tax measure passes; if it fails, they are cut in FY2028.

**Why the total budget still matters.** The $905 million is where the City's fixed obligations sit: pensions, retiree health, debt service, and the $1.8 billion of unfunded infrastructure the Auditor counts in the capital program. Those costs do not show up in the General Fund deficit, but they are paid by the same residents. A balanced General Fund and a City that is borrowing from its own future are both true at once.

*Sources: FY2027 & FY2028 Adopted Biennial Budget (June 23 2026 packet); FY2025 ACFR; City Auditor, Berkeley's Financial Condition FY2016–FY2025 (April 2026).*

**Explainer paragraph:**

The General Fund is the roughly one third of the City budget with no strings on it. Everything else — parcel-tax funds, enterprise funds like water and the marina, grants, bond proceeds — is restricted by the voters, the state, or the funder to a specific purpose. So "the deficit" is a General Fund story: $32 million projected for FY2027 before the balancing plan, against about $315 million in General Fund revenue. Property taxes supply about a third of that revenue; business, sales, utility and transfer taxes most of the rest. Two thirds of General Fund spending is people, and just over half is police and fire. The City cannot close a General Fund gap with parks-tax money or sewer fees; it can only cut General Fund programs, shift costs to other funds where the law allows, or raise General Fund revenue — which is what the November sales-tax measure is.

**Glossary entry (for berkeley-budget-glossary.html):**

*General Fund* — The City's main operating fund and the only large fund the Council may spend at its discretion. FY2027: ~$315M of the $905M all-funds budget. Structural-deficit figures, position cuts, and the proposed sales tax all refer to this fund alone.
