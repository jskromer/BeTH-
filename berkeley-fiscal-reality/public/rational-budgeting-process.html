A Rational Budgeting Process for Berkeley

A reform framework grounded in 54 audits, 384 recommendations, and the FY2027–FY2028 Proposed Budget.

Compiled June 2026  ·  Sources in berkeley-budget-database.json & audit-budget-opportunities.md
The conclusion is not "the numbers are fake." It is that the process produces an adopted budget that poorly predicts actual spending, defers its own structural fixes, and leaves the largest recurring cost driver to attrition. A rational process fixes the process, not just the year.

What the Data Shows

01The deficit is recurring — and closed with one-time money every year
$10M (2019) → $40M (2021) → $27M → $22M → $12M → $17M → $29M (2026). Every year was "balanced" with reserves, ARPA, fund balance, transfers, the Section 115 Trust, or vacancy savings. None resolved the structure.
02The baseline was padded
The FY27–28 budget re-based to actuals and cut the General Fund personnel budget by $16.5M / −7.4% (Salaries −$9.45M, Pension −$3.68M, Health/Retiree −$1.58M, Overtime −$0.71M). A budget that can shed $16.5M by "re-baselining to actual spending" was carrying $16.5M of appropriation that didn't reflect reality.
03Personnel is the structural driver — and is barely touched on the cost-sharing side
Salaries & benefits are 66–67% of the General Fund (FY27 proposed: Salaries $105.0M, Pension $54.6M, Health/Retiree $21.9M). The budget attacks this almost entirely through attrition (164 fewer positions) rather than the recurring cost of benefits per remaining employee.

And the City has a standing, ignored playbook for the rest: of 384 recommendations, 76 were dropped (many by silent 5-year lapse) and 43 are open — concentrated in the highest-spending departments (Public Works 33% implemented; City Manager 50%) and in exactly the categories a structural fix needs (Efficiency & Effectiveness 47%, Transparency & Accountability 29%).

Design Principles

1Truth in budgeting. The adopted budget should approximate what is actually spent. Minimize the adopted → amended → actual gap and publish it.
2Structural balance. Recurring revenue funds recurring expense; one-time money funds only one-time needs. No exceptions hidden in transfers.
3Outcome accountability. Every program states a goal and a measure; allocation follows results; audit findings feed the budget.
4Cost discipline on the real driver. Personnel cost is managed on both sides — workload-based positions and benefit cost-sharing, not attrition alone.
5Protected funds. Reserves and replacement / internal-service funds are rule-bound and cannot be silently raided.

The Process — Eight Mechanics, Each Tied to an Audit Revelation

1Baseline integrity (zero-based to actuals)CONFIDENCE: HIGH
Re-base every department to trailing actual expenditures annually; no automatic escalation of unspent lines. Vacant positions budgeted at true cost and removed after a fixed vacancy window unless re-justified.
AUDIT BASIS — The City just demonstrated this works — $16.5M came out of the GF personnel baseline. Institutionalize it instead of doing it once in a crisis.
2Carryover reform (appropriations lapse)DIRECTION HIGH · MAGNITUDE MEDIUM
Operating appropriations lapse at year-end. Only encumbered funds and separately-budgeted capital carry forward, by explicit re-appropriation with written justification. Capital lives in a multi-year fund so operating carryovers stop masking true operating cost.
AUDIT BASIS — The 2022 Financial Condition audit called for a capital / deferred-maintenance funding plan (dropped); commingling capital timing with operating budgets is part of why all-fund department totals swing wildly (Public Works $187M → $234M → $213M). Magnitude needs year-end fund-balance schedules + CAFR.
3AAO discipline (stop the mid-year drift)DIRECTION HIGH · MAGNITUDE MEDIUM
Consolidate Annual Appropriations Ordinance amendments to a fixed quarterly cycle. Each amendment must identify a specific source, be net-neutral to fund balance, or require a Council supermajority. Publish a standing adopted → amended → actual reconciliation.
AUDIT BASIS — The recurring "no performance measures / no documented procedures" theme (Zero Waste, Code Enforcement, Contracts audits) reflects a culture where money moves without a tracked rationale. AAO history/magnitude needs the AAO ordinances themselves.
4Personnel cost-sharing (manage the driver, not just headcount)LEVER HIGH · $ FIGURE LOW
Bargain employee contributions toward health-premium and pension cost across MOUs, phased in. The GF Health/Retiree line is $21.9M and Pension $54.6M; cutting 164 positions reduces count but leaves per-employee benefit cost — the actual growth driver — unaddressed.
AUDIT BASIS — Employee Benefits: Tough Decisions Ahead and the Financial Condition Section 115 Trust recommendation (dropped) both point here. This is a bargaining outcome, not a unilateral lever; no savings figure is credible until the active-vs-retiree health split and CalPERS contribution rates are in hand.
5Performance-based allocationCONFIDENCE: HIGH
Every program submits a goal and a measurable outcome with its budget request. Programs that cannot state a measure, or that miss targets, are flagged for review before funding is renewed — not cut automatically, but no longer funded on autopilot.
AUDIT BASIS — The single most repeated audit finding — missing performance measures in Zero Waste (2014/2016), Code Enforcement (2018), Library program evaluation (2020), Restaurant Inspections (2024), and Homeless Response Team (2025, all 6 recs still Not Started). The audits already wrote the requirement.
6Audit-accountability loop (end the silent 5-year lapse)CONFIDENCE: HIGH
The budget adoption packet reports, per department, the status of every open and relevant audit recommendation. Dropping a recommendation requires an explicit Council vote with a written cost-benefit — ending the practice where a fix is "dropped" simply because five years passed.
AUDIT BASIS — 76 of 384 recommendations dropped, a large share by automatic lapse rather than decision; the Financial Condition deficit fixes themselves were dropped "to allow for updated recommendations." Tie budget adoption to recommendation status and deferral becomes a recorded choice.
7Reserve and replacement-fund rulesCONFIDENCE: HIGH
Adopt a replenishment schedule for the Stability and Catastrophic reserves and protect internal-service / replacement funds: no diversion without a documented cost analysis showing the impact, approved by Council.
AUDIT BASIS — Fleet Replacement Fund Short Millions (2021) — 11 of 12 recs still only Partly Implemented while $2M (FY25) and $0.5M (FY24) were transferred out of the Equipment Replacement Fund; the dropped reserve-replenishment recommendation.
8Revenue integrityCONFIDENCE: HIGH
Maintain complete, current inventories of grants, leases, and fees; track receivables; set cost-recovery fees where allowed. Capture revenue the City is already owed before cutting services.
AUDIT BASIS — Citywide Grants Management (2016, all 15 dropped); 2009 Leases Audit Follow-Up (2025, lease inventory still not centralized 16 years on); franchise-fee recovery and fee-study items; the Business License Taxes audit ("…Will Increase Revenues").

Independent Assessment — Unvarnished

A large share of the gap is a governance choice.

The strongest claim here is not that the deficit is fake. There is real cost growth — 8% expense vs. 6.5% revenue over FY17–26 — that no process change fully erases. The defensible claim is narrower and more damaging: the City can re-baseline $16.5M when forced to; it has a standing audit playbook it mostly ignored in its biggest departments; and it has left the dominant cost driver — benefits per employee — to attrition rather than cost-sharing.

Two cautions: carryover and AAO discipline have legitimate limits (encumbrances and multi-year capital genuinely must carry — the reform is transparency and control, not literal "no carryover"); and health cost-sharing is bargained, not decreed, and can't be scored until the active/retiree split and contribution rates are sourced.

Data Still Needed to Quantify the Case

  • Year-end fund-balance and carryover schedules (CAFR / adopted budget book) — to size carryover.
  • FY2026 and prior Annual Appropriations Ordinances — to size mid-year AAO drift and build the reconciliation.
  • MOU benefit terms + CalPERS health schedules — to split active vs. retiree health and model contribution scenarios.
  • The Auditor's FY2025 updated financial condition report — where the dropped 2022 structural recommendations were said to be re-issued.
