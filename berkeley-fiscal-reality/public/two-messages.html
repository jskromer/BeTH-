Two Messages, One Set of Facts

Both messages rest on the same verified facts. They differ only in tone: the first uses the "fiction" frame to capture attention; the second is the measured version that names the two real problems and casts the City Auditor as the ally. Use them together — the first to get heard, the second to be trusted.

These are advocacy framings — one community member's way of communicating the data, not a neutral finding of the Transparency Hub. The underlying facts are sourced at the bottom.

MESSAGE 1To capture attention
Berkeley's budget is a fiction — and the City's own Auditor has the receipts.

Every year for the better part of a decade, Berkeley has declared its budget "balanced." Every year it was nothing of the kind. The gap has been papered over with reserves, ARPA money, fund balance, and one-time transfers — $10M, then $40M, then $27, $22, $12, $17, and $29M. Seven years of "balanced" budgets that never balanced anything.

How do we know the numbers aren't real? Because the moment the City was forced to re-base this budget to what it actually spends, $16.5 million in personnel costs simply vanished — money budgeted year after year against padded baselines. A budget you can shrink by $16.5M just by being honest was never an honest budget.

And this year's "surplus"? It depends on a sales-tax measure voters haven't passed yet. Strip out that assumption and the one-time money — which drops to zero next year — and the structural hole is right where it always was.

Meanwhile the City Auditor has handed Council a playbook: 384 recommendations across 54 audits. The City dropped 76 of them — many by letting them expire untried after five years — and has another 43 still open. The worst offenders are the departments with the most money: Public Works has acted on barely a third of its findings; the City Manager's office on half.

This isn't a math problem. It's a discipline problem. The two things Berkeley actually needs are simple to name and hard to dodge: tell the truth about the money, and hold programs accountable for results. The Auditor has been saying so for years.

MESSAGE 2To build the coalition
Berkeley's budget challenge is real — and two governance fixes would address most of it.

Berkeley faces a roughly $30-million-a-year General Fund gap. Some of it reflects genuine cost growth: over the last decade, expenses rose about 8% a year against 6.5% revenue growth. That part is real, and no amount of process reform erases it entirely.

But a large share of the problem is not the math — it's the process. Two issues stand out, and the City Auditor is our strongest ally on both.

First, transparency and honesty in budgeting. The adopted budget is a poor guide to what the City actually spends. This year's re-basing removed $16.5M from the personnel budget alone. The fix is straightforward: budget to actuals, let operating appropriations lapse, separate capital from operating, discipline mid-year amendments, and publish a standing "adopted-versus-actual" reconciliation.

Second, accountability to results. The most common finding across the Auditor's 54 audits is that programs lack measurable goals — from zero waste to code enforcement to homeless services. Of 384 recommendations, 76 were dropped and 43 remain open, concentrated in the highest-spending departments. The fix is to tie budgeting to performance, and to require an explicit Council decision with a cost-benefit before any recommendation is dropped.

This is exactly the work the Auditor's office exists to do. Council should treat the audit record as the backbone of the budget process, not a separate report filed and forgotten. Honesty about the money and accountability for results won't close the entire gap — but they would end the annual ritual of one-time fixes and rebuild public trust in the numbers.

Shared Fact Base

  • Recurring GF deficits closed with one-time measures: $10M (2019), $40M (2021), $27M (2022), $22M (2023), $12M (2024), $17M (2025), $29M (2026).
  • Re-baselining to actuals cut GF personnel budget $16.5M / −7.4% (Salaries −$9.45M, Pension −$3.68M, Health/Retiree −$1.58M, Overtime −$0.71M).
  • FY28 revenue assumes the Nov 2026 Sales & Use Tax measure passes; one-time resources fall from $2.1M (FY27) to $0 (FY28).
  • Cost growth FY17–FY26: ~8% expense vs. ~6.5% revenue.
  • Audit record: 384 recommendations, 54 audits; 265 implemented (69%), 76 dropped (20%, many by 5-year lapse), 43 open. Public Works 33% implemented; City Manager 50%.
  • FY2027 Audit Plan language: "ensuring City dollars are spent responsibly and aligned with Berkeley values."
