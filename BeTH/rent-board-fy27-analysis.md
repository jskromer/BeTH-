# Berkeley Rent Stabilization Board — FY 2026/27 Budget

**Source:** RSB Item 11B, June 18, 2026 — Resolution 26-23, "Adopting the FY 2026–2027 Budget, Staffing Model, Position Detail, and Maximum Expenditure Level." [PDF](https://rentboard.berkeleyca.gov/sites/default/files/2026-06/06-18-2026_RSB_ITEM_11B_ACTION_FY_2027_Budget_Staffing_Model_and_Expediture_Level.pdf)

**Bottom line up front:** This is a self-financed enterprise fund (Fund 801), funded entirely by registration fees on rental-property *owners*. It is walled off from Berkeley's General Fund and **does not contribute to the city's ~$29–33M structural deficit.** The direct, legal cost to renters is currently near zero — the registration-fee pass-through to tenants was not authorized for FY25-26 and is being wound down (High confidence).

## 2. Cost Burden to Renters

The honest answer is layered, because legal incidence and economic incidence diverge.

**Direct / legal incidence — essentially zero for renters today.** Every dollar of the $10.25M is levied on rental-property owners as a registration fee. Berkeley historically let owners pass a *portion* of the fee through as a capped monthly rent increase (e.g., $14/mo after the 2024-25 fee, $10/mo for pre-1999 tenancies in 2022-23), but the Board **did not authorize a pass-through for FY25-26, and the pass-through and its low-income reimbursement program are being wound down** (High confidence). On paper, the renter pays the Rent Board nothing.

**Per-unit cost level.** Spread across the ~27,500 regulated units the fees imply (21,500 fully covered + 6,000 partially covered), the program costs **~$363/unit/yr in fees, ~$411/unit/yr at the full FY27 authorization** (Moderate confidence — derived from dividing revenue/spend by implied unit counts). The owner of a fully covered unit pays $397/yr (~$33/mo).

**Economic incidence — shared and contested.** Standard public finance says statutory incidence rarely equals who actually bears a cost:

- For **sitting tenants** under a binding Annual General Adjustment cap, owners cannot raise the rent to recover the fee — so the short-run burden falls on the owner and is capitalized into property value. Renters in place are effectively shielded.
- For **new tenancies** (vacancy decontrol under Costa-Hawkins), owners reset to market and routine operating costs, including this fee, are embedded in the asking rent. Incoming renters bear a share.
- As a class, per-unit operating fees marginally raise the cost of supplying rental housing, which over time exerts mild upward pressure on rents and/or downward pressure on supply and maintenance — a diffuse, hard-to-quantify cost to renters generally (Low confidence on magnitude; direction is well-established).

**Net:** The Rent Board is best described as a **cost on rental-property owners (~$397/unit/yr), not a direct charge on renters.** The renter-side burden is indirect, capped by the very rent-control regime the fee funds, and currently minimized by the Board's decision to suspend the pass-through. Anyone claiming this board is a large direct cost to tenants is wrong on the current facts.

## 3. Comment on Its Value

**For the BeTH narrative, the single most important point:** this fund is self-financing and statutorily separate from the General Fund. It neither helps nor worsens Berkeley's structural deficit. It should be tracked as a distinct entity, not folded into the citywide cuts conversation.

**Fiscal health: strong, and deliberately so.** The FY27 "shortfall" of $1.05M is not distress — it's a planned drawdown of an over-target reserve. Reserves sit near 31% versus a 16% policy floor (roughly two months' operating expenses), leaving ~$3.58M at year-end even after the drawdown. The agency also beat FY24/25 budget by ~$987K on vacancy savings and over-collected revenue by ~$309K. This is a well-cushioned operation.

**The FY27 increase is front-loaded, not structural.** Of the +$3M jump, the bulk is one-time or strategic: ~$1.545M for the EcoMetricx data system replacing 3Di, $750K for Measure MM litigation, a new $500K capital reserve, and a $500K contingency. Recurring operations rose more modestly (~$9.25M). Reading the 36% headline as permanent growth would be a mistake.

**Cost efficiency: defensible but on the high side and rising.** ~$363–411/unit/yr to run registration, hearings, counseling, enforcement, and legal defense for 27,500 units with 31 FTE is plausible for a full regulatory apparatus, but it is not cheap, and staff themselves flag two structural cost drivers ahead: the 10-year lease escalating from ~$528K toward **~$750K by its final year**, and litigation that "is expected to continue well into FY 2026/27."

**The real risk is litigation, not the budget.** The Measure MM class action challenges the Board's authority to charge the partial-coverage fee. That fee base is $1.46M of FY27 revenue. An adverse ruling threatens both ~14% of revenue and potential refunds — a far bigger exposure than any line item in this budget (Moderate confidence; depends on case posture not disclosed here).

**Value verdict:** As a self-funded regulator delivering rent caps, just-cause eviction enforcement, free counseling, and a public registry, the Board offers real value to renters at no direct charge to them and zero burden on the General Fund. The budget is prudently reserved and the increase is largely one-time. The legitimate scrutiny points are (1) per-unit administrative cost that is rising faster than inflation, (2) an escalating lease the agency has locked in for a decade, and (3) the unhedged litigation exposure to its own fee structure.
