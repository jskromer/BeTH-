DTraceable threads — questions with a name attached

Each of these is answerable by a specific person or document, and none has been answered on the public record. Asking them is how a resident moves the debate off slogans.

1. Is there a dated HCD commitment, and what happens if Council misses it? Owens cites a December 2026 completion pledge; the May 2026 staff report cites none. Ask Jordan Klein or HCD's Housing Accountability Unit for the Program 27 text and HCD correspondence. If decertification is on the table, that changes the vote calculus; if it isn't, the urgency argument weakens.
Who: Klein (staff) · HCD HAU · the Housing Element itself (Program 27)
2. Where is the Strategic Economics pro forma? The Alternatives Report says "few rental projects in Berkeley are feasible in the current market" and that 4–8 stories is the sweet spot, but publishes no land values, rents, costs or inclusionary sensitivity. Without it, "1,518 units" is a zoning number, not a forecast.
Who: Strategic Economics via Raimi + Associates; Uttara Ramakrishnan (PM). Ask for the memo under the Public Records Act if it isn't posted.
3. Who sat on the Planning Commission ad hoc committee that produced the opportunity-sites pivot? The March 4 minutes don't say. The pivot cut capacity by 12–13% and exempted most shops; its authorship matters.
Who: Faye Messner (PC Clerk) · Emily Marthinsen (chair)
4. Have the named opportunity-parcel owners said anything? Property-owner meetings were reportedly delayed until Oct 29, 2025. The Andronico's, Wells Fargo and USPS parcels are the plan. USPS in particular does not sell quickly.
Who: Plan to Place (Dave Javid) for the stakeholder-interview log; owners directly; Solano BID and Elmwood BID for the merchant side.
5. Does the Elmwood historic-district application legally remove parcels from Option 1A? 28 properties on College between Russell and Webster; LPC split 3–3 and continued. A designation could collide with Program 27's fair-housing rationale — and with SB 330's anti-downzoning rule.
Who: LPC secretary; City Attorney; HCD if designation is used to reduce Housing Element capacity.
6. What did the Southside (2023) and Downtown (2012) upzonings actually produce, and for whom? Berkeley has two completed local experiments and a Rent Board registry. Nobody has published permits → completions → nearby rents for them. The corridor debate is being argued with Chicago and Minneapolis data instead.
Who: Rent Board data staff; Terner Center; a Goldman School or CED capstone team; the Build Berkeley database already has the project side.
7. What is the fiscal impact? No assessed-value or service-cost projection for the corridor rezoning has been published. New construction resets Prop 13 bases; unbuilt entitlements don't.
Who: City Budget Office; Alameda County Assessor (parcel base-year data); Council Budget & Finance Committee.
8. Which corridor parcels are already inside AB 2011 / SB 79 / AB 893 envelopes? If a North Shattuck parcel is within ¼ mile of Downtown BART, SB 79 already allows 75 ft there regardless of the Corridors vote. A parcel-level overlay map would shrink the argument to what is actually discretionary.
Who: City GIS; the Build Berkeley notebooks already geocode projects and could add the radii.

EWhere is this going? Berkeley in 10, 20 and 40 years

Nobody can forecast a city. But the direction is more legible than it looks, because the forces are slow, documented, and mostly already set. What follows is a reading of the record, not a prediction — and the confidence pills matter more here than anywhere else on the site.

What it will likely look and feel like

10 years (2036). Downtown and Southside unmistakably taller — a cluster of 20–30 story buildings around Downtown BART, 8–12 story blocks on Telegraph and Bancroft, most of them student-heavy. Three BART-lot neighborhoods under construction or newly occupied. Two to six new mid-rise buildings across the three corridors, probably on the Andronico's, post office, bank and supermarket lots; most storefronts unchanged. San Pablo Avenue visibly taller at a few nodes (75 ft base). ADUs in a large share of backyards. Hills unchanged. Moderate

20 years (2046). Two more RHNA cycles have passed, each with a higher number and fewer local exemptions. The corridors have filled in further where parcels were large; College Avenue still reads as a two-to-four story street with a few taller punctuations, unless the historic district froze it. Downtown's skyline is the East Bay's second after Oakland. Transit frequency, not zoning, is the binding constraint on further SB 79-type upzoning. Low

40 years (2066). Beyond any honest forecast. The one structural bet: the pattern of "density on corridors and transit, low-rise between" is baked into state law, the General Plan successor, and every capital investment since 2012, and it would take a political reversal at the state level to change it. Whether Berkeley is more or less affordable in 2066 depends on regional supply, not on what happens to the Elmwood 7-Eleven lot. Low

How a resident can actually watch it happen

  • Every April: read the Housing Element APR (Table B) — permits by income band. Ten minutes; the only official scoreboard.
  • Quarterly: open the Build Berkeley Datasette and sort by status. Completed vs. entitled is the real-world build-out rate.
  • Per project: type the address into Accela. Status, documents, and the date chain are all public.
  • Per decision: subscribe to corridors@berkeleyca.gov updates and the Planning Commission agenda; read "late correspondence" to see who is writing in and from where.
  • Per parcel: the GIS viewer and zoning map tell you what can legally be built next to you today.
  • Per law: the Terner Center's annual legislative round-up is the fastest way to learn what Sacramento just changed.
  • Per dollar: the BeTH budget pages for whether any of it shows up as revenue.
The honest answer to "where is this going" is: toward more state control of quantity, more local control of appearance, a much taller Downtown and campus district, three new BART neighborhoods, slow parcel-by-parcel change on the corridors, and a largely unchanged residential fabric everywhere else. The decisions still open in Berkeley are design standards, the inclusionary rate, tenant and small-business protections, and which specific parcels on three streets get 4–6 extra stories. That is a narrower fight than either side describes — and a more winnable one for residents who show up with the documents above.
