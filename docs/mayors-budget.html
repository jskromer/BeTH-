The “Mayor’s Budget” in Berkeley

Each budget cycle, the Mayor presents a set of budget recommendations. It looks like an executive’s budget — but Berkeley’s founding documents never created one. Here is what the Charter actually says, where the idea comes from, and whether the custom began with Loni Hancock.

Bottom line

It’s a custom, not a charter power.

The Charter assigns budget preparation to the City Manager and adoption to the Council. The Mayor holds one vote and no veto. The “Mayor’s budget” is a recommendation layered on top — influential, but not a founding-document instrument.

01

What you’re seeing at the budget meeting

When a Berkeley mayor “presents her budget,” she is not submitting the city’s budget. She is offering recommended changes to the budget the City Manager has already prepared, ahead of the Council’s adoption vote.

The instrument is real and recurring — in recent cycles the budget committee has formally taken up “council referrals and the mayor’s budget proposal” alongside the Manager’s plan. High But its weight is exactly one vote plus persuasion. It carries no veto and no automatic force.

What the Charter actually says

Berkeley’s “constitution” is its City Charter. Three provisions decide the question, and none of them mentions a Mayor’s budget. High

The Mayor is elected citywide (Charter §9), which gives her the only at-large mandate in city government — but directly elected is not the same as a strong mayor. Section 21 still limits her to presiding and a single vote, with no veto. High

03

Where the idea of a “mayor’s budget” comes from

The phrase carries real meaning — just from a different tradition than Berkeley’s. In strong-mayor cities (San Francisco, Oakland, Los Angeles), an elected chief executive proposes the budget and usually wields a line-item veto. That “executive budget” model grew out of Progressive-era reform a century ago. High

Berkeley adopted the competing model: council–manager government, in place since 1923. Here a professional, appointed manager prepares the budget and the elected council adopts it; the mayor presides. Berkeley’s analog to a strong mayor’s executive budget is simply the City Manager’s proposed budget. High

This was a deliberate choice, reaffirmed over decades. Berkeley’s Charter Review Committee explicitly rejected the strong-mayor form — including its veto power — on the principle that no single official should hold that concentration of authority. In the 1970s the city’s reform energy ran the other way entirely, toward a stronger council. High

04

So what is Berkeley’s “Mayor’s budget”?

A leadership instrument, not a legal one. Its real footing is positional and political:

  • Council chair. The Mayor leads the body that holds the adoption vote, which sets the framing for the whole debate.
  • Budget & Finance Committee chair. The Mayor currently chairs the three-member committee that reviews budget development — the natural launch point for mayoral recommendations.
  • Citywide mandate. As the only at-large elected official, the Mayor supplies an electorally accountable voice in a process otherwise run by an unelected manager.

None of these are charter powers. All of them are why an elected leader in a weak-mayor system still produces something that looks like an executive budget. Moderate

05

Provenance: did it start with Loni Hancock?

A persistent piece of local lore — relayed by former Mayor Jesse Arreguín — holds that the Mayor’s-budget tradition began with Loni Hancock, possibly in the 1970s. We tested it.

The dating fails for a mayoral budget. Hancock was a city councilmember from 1971–1979 and Mayor only from 1986–1994. She wasn’t mayor in the ’70s, so a Mayor’s budget can’t originate with her then. If it traces to Hancock at all, the window is her mayoralty — the late 1980s. High

There is a real 1970s budget reform — but a different one. As a councilmember, Hancock is credited with instigating citizen review of the budget and creating the consent calendar. That participatory-budget innovation is the likely seed of the “Hancock + 1970s + budget” memory — but it is not a mayor’s budget. Moderate

Her own oral history is silent. Hancock’s 2021 oral history (UC Berkeley / California State Government Oral History Program) was reviewed in full. Every budget passage concerns the state budget; her Berkeley mayoral years come up, but never in city-budget-process terms, and she makes no claim to have started the practice. The source neither confirms nor refutes it. High

So the attribution is plausible but unverified. No public document ties the practice’s origin to Hancock; the named “mayor’s budget proposal” appears in the record only in the recent era. For an undocumented custom, a former mayor’s testimony carries real weight on the who — but the when is most likely her mayoralty, c. 1986–1994, not the 1970s. Low–Moderate

Open record · help us close it

What we couldn’t verify

Berkeley’s online agenda and minutes archives don’t reach back to 1986–1994, so the origin of the Mayor’s-budget custom remains in oral tradition. Two things would settle it:

  • Offline records — physical Council minutes and budget messages from Hancock’s mayoral terms, held at the Berkeley Public Library, the City Clerk, or the Berkeley Historical Society.
  • Direct testimony — from Hancock (who gave a 2021 oral history) or from Arreguín, the original source of the claim.

Have a document, a minute entry, or a first-hand account? It belongs in the public record. Send it in and we’ll update this page with the source.
