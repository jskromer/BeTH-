What Homelessness Costs the City — and What That Number Hides

The reported ~$35M, every community-agency contract, how Berkeley compares to its neighbors, and the costs booked in other departments’ budgets that no one has added up. Compiled October 14, 2026.

Learn About Berkeley · built from public documents · residents decide
The headline: there is no single honest “cost per homeless person.” Divide the city’s reported ~$35M by the one-night count and you get ~$40,000 — the highest in the area. Divide it by the people the system actually serves in a year and it is plausibly $12,000–17,000. And the $35M itself is incomplete: the City Auditor found that most Homeless Response Team salaries are paid by the General Fund outside that total, and encampment-driven Fire/EMS, Public Works, and Parks costs sit uncounted in departmental budgets. All three statements are true at once. The number that matters — outcomes per dollar — is the one the city does not publish.

AThree denominators, three different answers

Every viral “per homeless person” number is the same $35M divided by a different crowd. Which denominator you pick changes the answer by 3×.

The same arithmetic trap runs the other way: a shelter bed is a 24/7/365 operation. In the contract data below, Berkeley’s interim motel shelters run $40–51k per client-year while the big congregate pathway center runs $12.4k — neither is comparable to institutions that operate 180 days a year.

CThe money map — five levels of government, one seam

The $35M is the city-visible slice of a system where money enters from five levels of government on different rules and timelines, and where control over placements sits mostly with the county. This is why no single document can add it up — and why every stream has its own politics. Companion: The Fund Map.

The seam: Berkeley pays for doors it mostly doesn’t control

Of the 320 shelter beds inside Berkeley, the city controls referrals to 56 (its ERF motels and CareBridge rooms). The other 264 fill through the county’s Coordinated Entry queue — average wait for permanent supportive housing 280 days, with fewer than a quarter of referrals housed within a year. The funnel, end to end: 25,000+ people touch the county system in a year → 8,201 counted countywide on one night (−13% since 2024) → 975 in Berkeley’s North County region (flat since 2024, while the county total fell) → 320 beds in the city → 56 city-referred. The county’s draft Home Together 2030 plan sets regional goals but prices them at $510M/yr of operations against a $242M baseline, with no funding source identified — and defers the document that would say what cities like Berkeley actually owe.

The cost ladder — why timing is the whole game

From the county’s own Home Together modeling, annual cost per household: prevention ≈ $7K · shelter ≈ $21K · permanent supportive housing ≈ $32K · resolving one encampment ≈ $4.7–5.4M per site (Berkeley’s three ERF grants) · CareBridge ≈ $267K per room over three years. Each step rightward costs roughly 3–4× the one before — and the county funds ~500 prevention households a year against a modeled need of 4,700.

Split incentives: who pays is not who saves

When the city pays for an intervention, most of the measurable savings (avoided ER visits, psychiatric bed-days, county crisis calls) land on Medi-Cal, hospitals, and the county — not on the city books that funded it. A BeTH working model of a 100-person high-needs cohort illustrates the shape: the city can come out ahead on its own slice while the county and Medi-Cal run net costs, or vice versa, depending entirely on who funds the intervention. The numbers are illustrative until the Section E data requests are filled — but the structure is the point: no ledger anywhere shows who pays versus who saves, which is exactly why prevention (the $7K rung) stays underfunded while crisis response (the $4.7M rung) gets grants.

  • No body sees the whole map. HSPE sees Measure P only; the Housing Advisory Commission sees the Housing Trust Fund and CDBG but never BHSA dollars; the Behavioral Health Commission holds the BHSA hearing but never sees the housing units; the Homeless Commission has listed no meeting since May 2022. The $36.1M BHSA plan passed on the consent calendar without discussion.
  • Don’t double-count. The ~$10M DHCS performance contract overlaps the BHSA allocation; county system totals include Berkeley pass-throughs. Any full-cost schedule (Section E) must net these out.

EWhat the $35M does not count

The Auditor’s own homeless-response audit (2025) says the reported total excludes most Homeless Response Team salaries, and that it did not assess other departmental costs. Those costs are real, booked in Fire, Public Works, and Parks budgets, and nobody has added them up.

The accounting rule a full-cost number needs: full municipal cost = reported program spending + genuinely incremental departmental costs + attributable General Fund labor and equipment + opportunity cost of displaced services − overlaps already paid from Measure P or other dedicated funds. Opportunity cost (the park maintenance that didn’t happen, the slower response elsewhere) is economically real but should be shown separately from cash outlays.

The data request that would close the gap — five items, all from existing city records:

  • Fire: 2024–2026 incidents coded as encampment-related, with crew and apparatus time, overtime and backfill.
  • Public Works / Zero Waste: encampment work orders, crew-hours, tonnage, disposal fees, contracts.
  • Parks: encampment work orders, staff-hours, repair spending, closures, deferred maintenance attributable to the diversion.
  • Finance / City Manager: a crosswalk showing which of those costs are already inside Measure P / HRT totals, so nothing is double-counted.
  • Auditor: a repeatable annual “full municipal cost of homelessness” schedule, reconciled to departmental ledgers, published alongside program spending and outcomes.

FClaims check: “$40k per homeless person vs. $25k per student”

A comparison circulating locally divides the $35M by the one-night count and sets it against Berkeley Unified’s per-pupil spending. Checked claim by claim:

ClaimVerdictWhat the record shows
Berkeley spends ~$35M/yr on homelessnessTrueThe city’s own figure (FY2024), >65% from Measure P. If anything it understates the full cost (Section D).
~864 homeless residentsRoughly true2026 PIT is 880 per press reporting (844 in 2024). But it is a one-night count, not the number of people served in a year.
Out of ~100,000 residentsWrongBerkeley’s population is 121,749 (ACS 2024). The unhoused share is 0.7%.
“$40k per homeless person vs. $25k per student”MisleadingThe $40k uses the one-night denominator (Section A); per person served it is $12–17k, under BUSD’s ~$25k. More fundamentally: the City of Berkeley does not fund BUSD at all. Schools run on state funds plus BUSD’s own voter-approved parcel taxes; homeless services run mostly on Measure P, which Berkeley voters dedicated to this purpose in 2018. Two governments, two voter-approved streams, no tradeoff between a shelter bed and a classroom.
“Leadership is hiding this”Half-trueThe spending is public. What is genuinely missing is results reporting: no unduplicated annual served count, no cost per exit to housing, no full-cost schedule — and the Auditor’s 2025 homeless-response recommendations were not started as of the last dashboard check. The scandal, if there is one, is the absence of outcome accounting, not the size of the per-person ratio.
