Following the money

The Marina Fund: one enterprise, six rescues

Berkeley set the Marina Fund up in 1964 to pay for the whole Waterfront out of berth rents and hotel and restaurant leases. It has run a structural deficit for 25 years. This page is the fund's ledger: every audited year, every dollar moved in from somewhere else, and what it owes.

Bottom line

Six pockets, one fund, no fix.

Since FY2022 the Marina Fund has been kept solvent with federal ARPA money, a State grant, a loan from the Workers' Compensation fund repaid out of hotel tax, a General Fund loan, General Fund transfers, and a cost shift to the Parks Tax. Every one went through Council. Every one was one-time, or shifted the cost to another fund. Staff's own forecast has the fund negative by FY2028, with $163 million of unfunded capital and no reserve.

01

How the fund was set up

Staff's words, September 2026: the fund was "established in 1964 to receive a $1.8M State loan to build the Waterfront," and "revenue from slips and hotel/restaurant leases was expected to cover the costs of debt service, maintenance and operations of the entire Waterfront." The entire Waterfront means 1.8 miles of streets, 11 parking lots, 5 miles of pathways, three parks including Cesar Chavez, 1,000 berths, the public docks and launch ramp, the office buildings, the restrooms, the Nature Center, Adventure Playground, 13 leases, and all the utilities. A berth-fee enterprise was asked to maintain a public park system. High

What about the hotel tax?

The Waterfront's hotel generates Transient Occupancy Tax, and the question comes up every time the Marina Fund is discussed: why not just give the Marina its own hotel tax? It is about the right size for the operating gap. It is not a fix, for three reasons.

Reason 1

It is already pledged.

Council's July 25, 2023 resolution directs the entire Waterfront TOT, from FY2025 until paid off, to repay the $5.5M Workers' Compensation loan plus $500,000. At $1.0–1.5M a year that runs four to six years, into roughly FY2029–2031. Whether any TOT has actually flowed to that repayment is an open question; the audits do not show it. High

Reason 2

It comes from the same hotel.

TOT is a percentage of room revenue at the Doubletree, which has lost money every year since 2020, is in special servicing, and has paid no rent since December 2025. When a hotel's lease income fails its tax yield is falling too; if the lender takes it or closes it, TOT goes to zero. Pledging TOT to the Marina swaps one bet on this hotel for a larger bet on the same hotel. High

Reason 3

It is General Fund money.

TOT is a general tax. Sending it to the Marina needs no vote, only a budget line, but it is a $1.0–1.5M annual General Fund subsidy with a label, in a year the General Fund is cutting $29M. It makes the sixth pocket permanent; it does not make the fund self-supporting. And at $1.5M a year, a capital reserve would reach the $163M need in about a century. High

Where the idea is strongest

Where the idea is strongest: 'revenue the Waterfront generates should stay at the Waterfront' is the charter fix this page points at, done properly. Waterfront parks to the Parks Tax (done, via Measure Y), Waterfront streets to the street funds, and Waterfront TOT to a Marina capital reserve once the loan is repaid, with the operating fund sized to berths and leases only. That would be a re-chartering, which is what the fund has needed for 25 years. It would still not be 'everything.' Moderate

What the ledger shows

Finding 1

Six different pockets in five years.

Federal ARPA (FY22–23), the Workers' Compensation internal-service fund (loan authorized 2023), a State capital grant (FY25), the General Fund (loan FY25; transfers FY26), the Parks Tax (cost shift from FY26), and waived indirect charges and Workers' Comp 'holidays' (FY25–26). None was the fund's own revenue. High

Finding 2

The fixes were one-time; the deficit is recurring.

The FY26 budget staff report says of its own fix: 'not sustainable long-term solutions.' Staff's September 2026 forecast shows the fund negative by FY28 and names a $1.045M gap with 'No General Fund available to bail out the Marina Fund.' High

Finding 3

The largest permanent fix came from a voter measure for parks.

Measure Y (Nov 2024) raised the Parks Tax; its first-year effect includes $1.579M of Waterfront park and landscape costs moved off the Marina Fund. The ballot question named parks, trees and landscaping generally. The Council's September 24, 2024 resolution, adopted before the vote, stated the intent to cover 'park and landscape maintenance in the Waterfront including Cesar Chavez.' The shift was disclosed, in a Council resolution rather than on the ballot. High

Finding 4

The 2023 loan is repaid by the General Fund, not the Marina.

Council's resolution of July 25, 2023 directs the $5.5M Workers' Compensation loan to be repaid from the Waterfront's Transient Occupancy Tax, which is General Fund revenue, plus a one-time $500,000. Staff estimated the General Fund loses $1.0–1.5M a year until it is paid. That subsidy does not appear on any Marina Fund slide. High

Finding 5

The two audits do not show the loan the way staff describe it.

Staff's May 2025 deck lists '$5.5M from Workers Comp fund for capital projects' under FY24. The FY2024 ACFR records no interfund loan receipt by the Marina fund and nothing due from it at June 30, 2024. The FY2025 ACFR records a $2,477,099 interfund loan receipt, shown as due to the General Fund. How the $5.5M was booked is an open question. High

Finding 6

The problem is the charter, not the year.

Every rescue restates the same mismatch: an enterprise fund that must cover streets, parks and a seawall for the general public out of boat slips and a hotel lease. Until the Waterfront's public-park costs are carried by the funds that carry every other park, the next rescue is already scheduled. Moderate

The counterargument

Strongest case against this page

An enterprise fund with a 25-year structural deficit has two honest outcomes: subsidize it openly or close the service. Berkeley has chosen to subsidize, and every transfer on this page went through a public vote of the Council. Measure Y's park-maintenance purpose does cover Waterfront parks, which are parks, and the Council said so before the election. The Doubletree collapse is a Bay Area hotel-market event the City did not cause. Replacing a State loan with an internal one saved the fund up to $10M in interest. And letting the Waterfront fail would cost more than any of this. The ledger does not settle that argument; it makes it visible. Moderate

  • Whether the FY2025 $11.13M 'charges for services' in the government-wide schedule includes the interfund loan; the proprietary statement shows $7.59M operating revenue.
