Berkeley's Adopted Plans vs. Its Strategic Priorities

All 27 plans listed under "Our Work → Adopted Plans" on berkeleyca.gov, mapped to the 2018 Strategic Plan's nine goals and today's budget reality. Compiled July 14, 2026.

Berkeley Transparency Hub · neutral tracker · residents decide
The headline: Berkeley's planning portfolio is barbell-shaped — a cluster of fresh, externally-driven plans (2020–2026) sits on top of a land-use and climate backbone that is 15–40 years old. Four of the nine Strategic Plan goals (fiscal health, customer service, workforce, standalone equity) have no adopted plan at all. The Strategic Plan itself is 8 years old, and its "refreshed each budget cycle" priorities have effectively been replaced by deficit management: ~$29M annual deficits, ~10% General Fund cuts, 138 positions slated for elimination in the FY2027–28 budget.

CCoverage gaps: goals with no plan

  • Goal 4 (financially healthy government) — no fiscal sustainability plan is listed, yet this is the city's dominant operational reality: projected deficits of $29.2M (FY27) and $29.5M (FY28), a proposed sales-tax measure, and General Fund cuts of ~10.3% (≈7.0% if the tax passes).
  • Goal 9 (workforce) — no plan, and the FY27–28 budget slates 138 positions for cuts, working directly against the goal.
  • Goal 8 (customer service) — no plan.
  • Goal 3 (equity) — no standalone plan; equity appears only as a lens inside newer plans (Pedestrian, Vision Zero, Adeline, Electrification Strategy).

Goal 1 (affordable housing) is nearly as thin: nothing on this list directly addresses housing production or homelessness. The real instruments — the Housing Element, Measure O bond, Measures P/W transfer-tax funding — live elsewhere on the city site.

DPatterns worth noting

Plans stay current only when something external forces it. The LHMP (FEMA mandate), CWPP (grant eligibility), and Bicycle/Pedestrian plans (competitive transportation funding) are fresh. Purely aspirational documents — Climate Action Plan, Resilience Strategy, the corridor plans — go stale because nothing compels renewal.

The strategic hierarchy is inverted. The documents that should sit at the top — the General Plan (2002) and Strategic Plan (2018) — are older than most of the tactical plans nominally implementing them. The site still describes priorities as "refreshed each budget cycle," but the budget cycle is now consumed by deficit closure, not strategic priority-setting; the February 2026 council referral re-prioritization is the closest thing to a current priority list.

Fiscal reality strands the old plans further. Whatever unfunded recommendations sit inside the 1990s corridor plans, the 2009 Pools and Climate plans, and SOSIP were already unlikely; a two-year budget cutting 10% of the General Fund makes them dead letters unless the 2026 infrastructure bond measure passes and absorbs some of the capital backlog.
