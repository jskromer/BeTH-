Berkeley’s Adopted Plans vs. Its Strategic Priorities

All 27 plans listed under “Our Work → Adopted Plans” on berkeleyca.gov, mapped against the nine goals of the 2018 Strategic Plan, the city’s current fiscal position, and the referral queue that decides which plans staff actually work on.

In short

The portfolio is barbell-shaped, and the hierarchy is upside down.

A cluster of recent, externally-driven plans sits on top of a land-use and climate backbone that is 15 to 40 years old, with little in between. The two documents that should sit at the top — the General Plan (2002) and the Strategic Plan (2018) — are older than most of the tactical plans nominally implementing them.

Four of the nine strategic goals have no adopted plan at all. And the plans that do exist reach staff only through a separate ranked queue of council referrals, where three of the four steps can stop a project without anyone voting against it.

Framework

The accountability pipeline

A plan is one stage of a five-part loop that runs from what the city says it will do to what independently gets checked. Each artifact on this page — and its companion trackers — is one stage. The loop leaks at every handoff.

StageArtifactThe question it answersWhere it stands
1 · IntentThe 27 adopted plans and the nine Strategic Plan goalsWhat will we do?12 of 27 stale or defunct; four goals have no plan. Sections A–C.
2 · PrioritizationThe council referral queue (Reweighted Range Voting)Which intents become scheduled work?Three of four steps can stop a project by inaction; breadth is penalised. Section F.
3 · FundingThe FY27–28 biennial budgetWhich work gets dollars?A deficit year turns budgeting into triage; a top rank is not an appropriation. Section C.
4 · OutcomeThe Annual Comprehensive Financial Report (ACFR)What actually happened?The audited year-end actuals — the record the adopted budget is measured against. See below.
5 · VerificationThe City Auditor’s 384 tracked recommendationsDid it work? Were the fixes made?76 formally dropped; the loop rarely returns to a plan. Audit tracker.

The through-line: nothing binds one stage to the next. A plan reaches staff only through the queue (1→2); a ranked referral is not funded (2→3); the adopted budget balances on paper while the ACFR records what the year actually did (3→4); and the Auditor’s findings carry no budget consequence (4→5), so the loop rarely closes back to stage 1. The one force that reliably closes a handoff is external compulsion — a FEMA mandate, a federal consent decree, grant eligibility. What is left to internal discipline degrades at several stages at once. High

Stage 4 · what the actuals show

The adopted budget is a projection; the ACFR is the audited record of what the year did. In FY2025 the General Fund moved from roughly a +$2.0M adopted position to about −$16.7M in actuals — the distance between the budget as passed and the year as lived. It is the FY2025 ACFR that the City Auditor’s April 2026 Financial Condition review drew on to conclude the gap is recurring rather than one-time. This is a recurring decision made under constraints, not an inevitability. Moderate — budget-vs-actual swing from the Hub’s compilation of the audited ACFRs; verify against the ACFR budgetary-comparison schedule.

C

Coverage gaps: goals with no plan

  • Goal 4, financially healthy government. No fiscal sustainability plan is listed, though this is the city’s dominant operational reality: projected deficits of $29.2M in FY27 and $29.5M in FY28, and General Fund cuts of roughly 10.3% — about 7.0% if the proposed sales tax passes. High See the companion Plans × Audits × Budget tracker, which maps each goal to the City Auditor’s recommendation backlog.
  • Goal 9, workforce. No plan, and the FY27–28 budget slates 138 positions for elimination — working directly against the stated goal. High
  • Goal 8, customer service. No plan.
  • Goal 3, equity. No standalone plan. Equity appears as a lens inside newer documents — the Pedestrian Plan, Vision Zero, Adeline, the electrification strategy — but nothing anchors it.

Goal 1, affordable housing, is nearly as thin. Nothing on the adopted-plans list directly addresses housing production or homelessness. The real instruments — the Housing Element, the Measure O bond, Measures P and W transfer-tax funding — live elsewhere on the city site and are not presented as plans.

Where the missing plans matter most is in contracting. A plan adopted by resolution cannot be enforced against the city — that is the argument in Section F1. But a contract can be enforced against a provider. Contracting is the one place in this system where the city holds real leverage, and there is no adopted plan governing how it uses it.

Stated position
“Government should require outcome-based contracting and standardized public reporting. Every publicly funded provider should track activity, placement, retention, stability, cost, and failure.”
Steve Kromer · Berkeley Transparency Hub

Six measures. Public reporting in this space reliably captures the first and the fifth — how many people were served, and what it cost. The middle three describe whether the service worked. The last one is the measure almost no jurisdiction publishes.

  • Activity
  • Placement
  • Retention
  • Stability
  • Cost
  • Failure

Why it belongs on this page: outcome terms written into a contract are enforceable in a way a resolution-adopted plan is not. Standardized reporting is what makes an aspirational goal auditable. Goals 3, 4, 8 and 9 have no plan behind them — and no reporting standard either, which is why their absence is hard to see from the outside.

D

Three patterns

Plans stay current only when something external forces it

The Local Hazard Mitigation Plan has a FEMA mandate. The wildfire plan is tied to grant eligibility. The Bicycle and Pedestrian plans are tied to competitive transportation funding. All are fresh. The purely aspirational documents — the Climate Action Plan, the Resilience Strategy, the 1990s corridor plans — go stale, because nothing compels renewal and no one can be held to them.

The strategic hierarchy is inverted

The documents that should sit at the top are the oldest. The city still describes its priorities as refreshed each budget cycle, but the budget cycle is now consumed by deficit closure rather than priority-setting. The February 2026 council referral re-prioritization is the closest thing Berkeley has to a current, ranked statement of what it intends to do. Section F takes that list seriously.

Fiscal reality strands the old plans further

Whatever unfunded recommendations sit inside the 1990s corridor plans, the 2009 pools and climate plans, and SOSIP were already unlikely. A two-year budget cutting 10% of the General Fund makes them dead letters unless the November 2026 infrastructure bond passes and absorbs part of the capital backlog. Moderate

F

How an adopted plan actually reaches staff

Adoption commits the city rhetorically, and sometimes legally. It commits no staff hours and no dollars. Everything on this page has to pass through one mechanism to become work, and that mechanism is not on the plans page.

F1 — The second axis: enforceability

Sections B and C grade these plans by currency. There is a second axis that changes what staleness means: legal force. A stale plan that binds the city is a different problem from a current plan that merely guides it.

Cross the two axes and the pattern sharpens. The General Plan is 24 years old and it is the document all zoning must conform to. The Waterfront Master Plan is 40 years old and governs an area now under active specific-plan work. Meanwhile the newest plan on the list, the June 2026 Bicycle Plan, sits in the tier with the least legal force. Berkeley’s binding documents are its oldest, and its current documents are its weakest.

F2 — Four decisions, three of which can be made by doing nothing

Under the City Charter, Council cannot direct staff informally. Article VII §28(b) requires that Council and its members deal with the administrative service solely through the City Manager, and bars any member from giving orders to the Manager’s subordinates. So the only lawful route from a plan to scheduled work is a collective vote on a referral — which is not an ordinance, not an appropriation, not a contract award, and not a project approval for environmental review purposes. It binds no future council and creates no right anyone can enforce. High

A referral is filed and adopted
Usually through a policy committee first. The item joins the outstanding-referral list. Nothing is funded and no work is scheduled.
It is scored 0 to 5 by nine officials and ranked
Once a year, by Reweighted Range Voting. A low rank is not a rejection — the item simply sits.
The City Manager may reorder it
Every adopting resolution grants the Manager authority to assign referrals so as not to exceed departmental staff capacity, explicitly including assignment out of the approved order. This is not a denial either.
Money has to arrive separately
The rankings only guide the Manager and the Budget & Finance Committee in developing Strategic Plan projects and funding allocations. An appropriation that never materialises is not a vote against the project.

Only the first step is a decision anyone has to defend. The three marked in red are places where a project stops because nothing happened.

F3 — How the ranking works, and what it rewards

Berkeley has used Reweighted Range Voting since March 2016. Each member scores every referral 0 to 5, with no limit on repeat scores. The highest total becomes priority one. Then the remaining scores are reweighted downward in proportion to the influence each member has already spent, so a member who scores many referrals highly exhausts their weighted vote quickly and has diminishing influence over what follows. Unscored items default to zero. The design goal is proportionality — a council minority still gets some priorities served rather than being shut out by a bare majority. High

The side effect is that breadth is penalised. Scoring a whole plan’s worth of items highly is self-defeating. In 2025, the last year individual ballots were published, one official scored only four of 43 referrals, all fives, and placed all four in the top seven; another scored 42 of 43 in the two-to-five range and spent their weighted vote almost immediately. Whether that reflects strategy or simply differing breadth of interest cannot be read off a score sheet. Moderate

Records note

The 2025 results package (March 25, 2025, Item 3) included an attachment with every member’s raw scores by district. The 2026 package (February 10, 2026, Item 1) does not — it publishes the ranked list only. Individual ballot behaviour is no longer visible in the current cycle. High

F6 — Three fixes available without a charter amendment

  • Bundle by plan. Let Council score plan-implementation programmes rather than a dozen fragments of the same plan. The ranking system can handle a consolidated item as easily as a fragment.
  • Tier the queue. Referrals implementing legally binding obligations — specific plans, state mandates — should not compete on the same 0-to-5 scale as discretionary pilots.
  • Publish the override, and restore the ballots. The Manager’s reordering authority is granted every year and its exercise is never reported. Individual scores were published in 2025 and dropped in 2026. Both are one-page disclosures.

What we could not verify

  • Whether the Manager’s reordering authority is ever used. It is granted in every adopting resolution. We have found no report disclosing when referrals were worked out of rank order. Absence of a record is not evidence the authority is unused.
  • General Plan conformity review of public works. State law contemplates an annual review. Whether Berkeley performs one, and what it produces, we have not established.
  • The verbatim adopted Hopkins motion. The July 28 outcome is confirmed (7–2 to advance; 5–4 rejecting alternative routes — see F5). The exact adopted text and any attached conditions await the published minutes; no funding source was identified in the reporting.
  • Which 14 referrals were removed in January 2026, and on what basis.
