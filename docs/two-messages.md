# Two Messages, One Set of Facts

Both messages below rest on the same verified facts (sources at the bottom). They differ only in tone: Message 1 uses the "fiction" frame to capture political attention; Message 2 is the measured version that names the two real problems and casts the City Auditor as the ally. Use them together — the first to get heard, the second to be trusted.

---

## Message 1 — "The Budget Is a Fiction" (to capture attention)

**Berkeley's budget is a fiction — and the City's own Auditor has the receipts.**

Every year for the better part of a decade, Berkeley has declared its budget "balanced." Every year it was nothing of the kind. The gap has been papered over with reserves, ARPA money, fund balance, and one-time transfers — $10 million, then $40 million, then $27, $22, $12, $17, and $29 million. Seven years of "balanced" budgets that never balanced anything.

How do we know the numbers aren't real? Because the moment the City was forced to re-base this budget to what it *actually spends*, **$16.5 million in personnel costs simply vanished** — money that had been budgeted year after year against padded baselines that bore no relationship to reality. A budget you can shrink by $16.5 million just by being honest about it was never an honest budget.

And this year's "surplus"? It depends on a sales-tax measure voters haven't passed yet. Strip out that assumption and the one-time money — which drops to **zero** next year — and the structural hole is right where it always was.

Meanwhile the City Auditor has handed Council a playbook to fix this: **384 recommendations across 54 audits.** The City **dropped 76 of them** — many simply by letting them expire untried after five years — and has another 43 still open. The worst offenders are the departments with the most money: Public Works has acted on barely a third of its audit findings; the City Manager's office on half.

This isn't a math problem. It's a discipline problem. Berkeley doesn't have a budget — it has an annual ritual of one-time fixes and quiet deferrals. The two things it actually needs are simple to name and hard to dodge: **tell the truth about the money, and hold programs accountable for results.** The Auditor has been saying so for years. It's time the rest of City Hall listened.

---

## Message 2 — Two Fixable Problems (to build the coalition)

**Berkeley's budget challenge is real — and two governance fixes would address most of it.**

Berkeley faces a roughly $30-million-a-year General Fund gap. Some of it reflects genuine cost growth: over the last decade, expenses rose about 8% a year against 6.5% revenue growth. That part is real, and no amount of process reform erases it entirely.

But a large share of the problem is not the math — it's the process. Two issues stand out, and the City Auditor is our strongest ally on both.

**First, transparency and honesty in budgeting.** The adopted budget is a poor guide to what the City actually spends. This year's re-basing to real expenditures removed $16.5 million from the personnel budget alone — proof that prior budgets carried padding that didn't reflect actual need. Year-end carryovers and mid-year appropriation amendments widen the gap between what Council adopts and what is ultimately spent. The fix is straightforward: budget to actuals, let operating appropriations lapse, separate capital from operating, discipline mid-year amendments, and publish a standing "adopted-versus-actual" reconciliation so residents can see the difference.

**Second, accountability to results.** The single most common finding across the Auditor's 54 audits is that programs lack measurable goals and outcome tracking — from zero waste to code enforcement to homeless services. Of 384 recommendations, 76 were dropped (many by simply lapsing after five years) and 43 remain open, concentrated in the highest-spending departments. The fix is to tie budgeting to performance: every program states a goal and a measure, and dropping an audit recommendation requires an explicit Council decision with a cost-benefit — not a silent five-year expiration.

This is exactly the work the Auditor's office exists to do, and its FY2027 plan commits to "ensuring City dollars are spent responsibly and aligned with Berkeley values." Council should treat the audit record as the backbone of the budget process, not a separate report filed and forgotten. Honesty about the money and accountability for results won't close the entire gap — but they would end the annual ritual of one-time fixes and rebuild public trust in the numbers.
