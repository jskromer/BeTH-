Train Horns & Quiet Zones: The 20-Year Project Nobody Owns

West Berkeley hears a federally mandated 96–110 decibel horn, day and night, at every open rail crossing. A federal process exists to silence routine horns. Berkeley bought a $500K study, banked a credit, watched the county fund $100M+ of crossing work next to the problem — and has no quiet zone planned, funded, or queued.

Berkeley Transparency Hub · built from public documents · residents decide
Why the horns blow: The federal Train Horn Rule (49 CFR Part 222, effective 2005) requires every train to sound its horn — two long, one short, one long, at 96–110 dB — beginning 15–20 seconds before every public at-grade crossing. This is not an engineer's choice and not a City rule. The only lawful way to stop routine horns is a federally recognized quiet zone, which only a public authority (the City) can establish, and only after paying for safety measures that replace the horn's risk reduction. AWhat a quiet zone actually is

A designated stretch of track — at least one-half mile, covering one or more consecutive crossings — where trains stop sounding horns routinely (emergency use continues). The City must first make each crossing as safe without horns as it was with them.

ElementRequirementSource
Who can establish oneOnly the public authority responsible for the roads — the City of Berkeley. Not Union Pacific, not Amtrak, not residents.FRA
Approved safety measures (SSMs)Four-quadrant gates, medians/channelization ≥60 ft, one-way conversion with gates, or permanent closure.FRA
Risk mathThe corridor's Quiet Zone Risk Index must be brought at or below nationwide/with-horn thresholds using FRA's calculator.FRA
Process~10 steps: diagnostic reviews with railroad and CPUC, updated crossing inventory, 60-day Notice of Intent, construction, signage, Notice of Establishment.FRA / CPUC
State roleNo state approval required; CPUC's rail branch comments on safety and processes any physical crossing alteration (GO 88-B).CPUC
Who paysThe City — engineering, construction, and perpetual maintenance of the railroad's warning devices, plus reimbursing UP for every meeting and review.Union Pacific

Union Pacific's official position: it opposes quiet zones as a safety matter but must cooperate when a city follows the federal process. High

CBerkeley's timeline — two decades of almosts

2005. Federal Train Horn Rule takes effect nationwide; the quiet-zone mechanism is created. Cities across the country begin applying. High
Jan 22, 2019. Council authorizes a $500,000 contract with TranSystems Corporation for "Preliminary Engineering and Environmental Clearance" on a Railroad Crossing Safety Improvement/Quiet Zone project (term Feb–Dec 2019). No completed feasibility study, cost estimate, or next-step report from that contract has surfaced in the public record BeTH has reviewed. High on the contract; Unknown on what the City got for it.
2020. As part of the I-80/Gilman interchange project, Council permanently closes the Camelia Street crossing to vehicles (Ord. 7738-NS, BMC 14.24.240). The code section says the closure lets the City "bank credits towards a possible railroad quiet zone in the future." A closure is the strongest SSM there is — this is a real, on-the-books down payment. High
2022. Emeryville pursues quiet zones at its three northern crossings ($10–12M grant strategy); its mayor flags that the scope could take in a Berkeley-border crossing Berkeley "might independently pursue." No joint project materializes. High
2023–2026. Alameda CTC's Rail Safety Enhancement Program Phase A advances: 28 crossings countywide, five in Berkeley (Virginia, Hearst, Cedar, Addison, Bancroft) — pedestrian gates, swing gates, channelizing railings, medians, fencing, ADA improvements. Funded by Measure BB, Regional Measure 3, SB1 TCEP ($16.6M), and a $25M federal CRISI grant. Berkeley public meeting May 22, 2024; first construction (Livermore/Hayward) began June 2026, completion targeted Summer 2028. Program documents give Phase A costs of $75.2M (2023 TCEP baseline) to ~$118M (current CTC estimate). High
Sept 9, 2024. Berkeleyside's headline states the position exactly: "New safety measures, but no 'quiet zones,' planned for West Berkeley rail crossings." Alameda CTC is explicit on its program page: "Alameda CTC's RSEP is not part of the City of Berkeley's railroad quiet zone study efforts." The county builds safety hardware; nobody is running the federal quiet-zone process. High
Oct 2023 – Dec 2025. News outlets report five people killed by trains in West Berkeley: two in October 2023, one at Potter/Bay in December 2023, one in December 2024, one near Bancroft in December 2025. Most were people on the tracks between crossings — deaths a quiet zone would not address and gates/fencing might. The safety and noise problems overlap but are not the same problem. Moderate
Feb 2026. The Council's 2026 referral prioritization — the queue that decides what staff work on — contains no quiet-zone, train-horn, or rail-crossing referral. Whatever the 2019 contract started, it has no owner, no sponsor, and no place in line. High

DThe open question worth asking now

RSEP-A is about to install, at county and state expense, much of the physical hardware — medians, gates, channelization — that a quiet-zone application would otherwise have to buy. That changes the math.

The FRA's quiet-zone calculator scores each crossing's risk with its installed equipment. Crossings that emerge from RSEP-A with four-quadrant-equivalent protection and channelized medians may need little or no additional capital to qualify — the remaining cost could be closer to the process itself: diagnostics, the risk analysis, UP reimbursements, signage, and the recurring maintenance agreement. Add the already-banked Camelia closure credit and Berkeley's marginal cost of a quiet zone may never be lower than the day RSEP-A finishes in Berkeley.

Nobody has published that gap analysis. It is a bounded, answerable staff question: after RSEP-A, what exactly would each of the six crossings still need to meet the FRA risk threshold, and what would it cost? The 2019 study money was supposed to buy an answer like this; seven years later, the question is cheaper to answer than it has ever been.

The counterargument is also real: Union Pacific opposes quiet zones on safety grounds, this corridor has killed five people in 26 months, and horns exist because they work. A city that pursues quiet zones takes on both the liability posture and the maintenance bill. That trade-off is a policy choice for residents and Council — BeTH's point is only that the choice is currently being made by default, without the analysis. High on positions; the weighing is yours.
