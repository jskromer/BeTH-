# Aquatic Park: The Lagoon, the Weir and the Tide Tubes

*Learn About Berkeley, companion text to the web page berkeley-aquatic-park.html. Confidence labels: High = read from the cited primary document or computed from the City's own data; Moderate = secondary source, single private source, field observation, or inference; Low = single unverified source or absence of evidence; Unknown = not retrieved. Last updated November 27, 2026.*


Berkeley's largest park is a strip of San Francisco Bay that a 1930s highway cut off and a federal relief program turned into a water park. Its water comes from two places: the Bay, through five 24-inch pipes under I-80, and the City's storm drains, through two overflow connections and a row of street outfalls. A small structure under University Avenue at 2nd Street decides when the biggest of those storm drains spills into the lagoon. This page tells the story, explains the weir, reads the 2019–2021 bacteria sampling, and follows the money.

## A. The short story

**The lagoon is an accident of highway building.** In the mid-1930s the State's Eastshore Highway (then US 40, now I-80) was built on a causeway of fill across the tidal flats off West Berkeley. The causeway cut off a strip of bay bottom, and that strip was also dug for fill, leaving a long narrow trench along its western edge. The City asked for federal relief money in 1935, and the Works Progress Administration turned the cut-off water into a mile-long lagoon with rock walls, a model-yacht basin and tide tubes, dedicated in May 1937. **\[High\]** on the mechanism (the City's own 2008 APIP study); **\[Moderate\]** on dates and builder, which the sources give differently (see Section C).

**Everything about the park's water follows from that origin.** The lagoon sits below the level of the City and beside the Bay, so it is the low point that both want to fill. Bay water can only get in through pipes under the freeway, and those pipes have been failing for decades. Storm water gets in three ways: directly from a row of east-side street outfalls; from the Potter Street drain at the south end, which is tidal and connected to the Model Yacht Basin; and from the Strawberry Creek drain at the north end, which is supposed to send water to the lagoon only in large storms and the highest, surge-raised tides. The thing that enforces "only in large storms" is a weir inside the Strawberry Creek storm drain under University Avenue at 2nd Street: a concrete crest topped, until December 2019, by a 12-inch redwood board, and since then by a metal plate. **\[High\]** (APIP 2008; the City's April 2022 water-quality fact sheet; City CCTV report, January 2021; Steve Kromer's 2019 files).

**In August 2019 the system failed in the way the weir is meant to prevent.** The lagoon rose about ten inches in dry weather, went opaque, its widgeon grass died, and tested far above the State bacteria standard. Volunteers filmed water pouring in at the Addison Street outfall. The City's own account, published in 2022: in early November 2019 "residents helped staff identify water coming into the Aquatic Park Lagoon via the Strawberry Creek storm drain overflow at high tide during dry weather," staff "discovered that the old 12-inch high redwood weir in the University Avenue storm drain had disappeared, thereby allowing water from the Bay to enter Aquatic Park during high tides," and on December 10, 2019 Public Works "replaced the old redwood weir with a metal weir" and the water level returned to normal. Weekly sampling then ran for sixteen months; the lagoon came back below the standard in dry weather and went over it again after most significant rains, a pattern that has continued through 2026. **\[High\]** (City fact sheet, April 2022; Regional Water Board Executive Officer's report, February 5, 2020; the City's results tables, reproduced below).

**Money has arrived, slowly, from four directions.** Measure T1 and the Parks Tax paid to clean the five tide tubes in 2020. A \$1.5M EPA grant, matched by the Clean Storm Water fund, is paying for green infrastructure and trash capture on the east side; that project's total has nearly doubled in the CIP and goes to bid in January 2027. A State transportation grant of roughly \$4M, matched by \$800,000 from the General Fund, is to replace the tide tubes. And nothing yet pays for the \$50M idea in the Potter watershed, the \$5.8M east-side drainage rebuild, or the \$8.3M playground master plan that sit on the Parks Department's unfunded list. **\[High\]** (City CIP, Council items and PRW unfunded-projects lists; Section F).

**The counterargument.** The park has never been in better hydraulic shape than it is now. The five main tubes were cleared and repaired in 2020–2022; the City Manager reported no flooding during the record January 2023 storms, more fish than before, and no widgeon grass or algae. The weir worked as designed once the board was back. Two grants and a General Fund match are in hand. On that reading, the 2019 episode was a maintenance lapse that was found and fixed, not a structural failure, and the page's weight on it is a stakeholder's view. The Water Board's own report credits the City with investigating sewers, storm drains, encampments and the grass-cutting contractor, and fixing the weir as "a first step." The reply: the City's own T1 evaluation said the main tubes are clay, failing, and would reach "complete failure in no later than 10 years from 2020"; the 2008 study's tidal-exchange fix (115 acre-feet per cycle instead of 22) has never been built; and the board that governs a 2,000-acre watershed's overflow into a public lagoon was found missing three months after the water rose, with residents' help, after the City had first attributed the rise to the tide tubes. Both readings are in the record.

## C. How the park was built, and why the lagoon is where it is

### The causeway and the borrow pit

Before the 1930s the site was open bay: early charts show "sub- and intertidal bay bottom," and the old shoreline is now the main lagoon's east edge. The State's highway was laid on a fill causeway a few hundred feet offshore. The City's 2008 APIP study puts it plainly: the freeway alignment "cut off a portion of the bay," and "the lagoons were used as borrow areas for the freeway construction." The deeper water is a long, narrow trench along the lagoon's west side where the fill was dug. So the answer to "cut-off mudflat or dug pit?" is both: isolated by the causeway, then deepened by excavation. **\[High\]**

The Southern Pacific embankment to the east is older than the park; its eucalyptus were planted to hold the embankment, and it forms the park's east boundary rather than the lagoon's cause. Who owned the tidelands is less clear: Friends of Five Creeks' aquaticpark.org says the Santa Fe held them and traded them; Finacom reports the City converting a railroad lease on 2.76 acres into an option at \$750 an acre in December 1937. **\[Low\]** on ownership.

A 2023 City worksession on the Waterfront Specific Plan calls the lagoon a highway "excavation (borrow) pit" the City chose not to fill. The City Manager's 2023 memo calls it "this artificial lagoon created as part of the construction of I-80 in the 1930s." **\[High\]** that this is the City's own account.

### What the WPA built

A mile-long lagoon with rock shoreline walls; the Model Yacht Basin with concrete-and-rock terraces and stone bleachers; a road; about 10,000 trees and shrubs by 1939; at least one boathouse; and the tide tubes. The Living New Deal describes it as a "water park for water skiing, canoeing, sculling and model yacht racing." The 1937 pageant ran outboard and inboard motorboat, sail, rowing and shell races. Hydroplane racing, often mentioned in passing, is not found in any source consulted. **\[Moderate\]**

Today the park is 102 acres: 68.0 acres of open water in three lagoons (the main lagoon, the Model Yacht Basin and the Radio Tower Pond), 0.7 acres of salt or brackish wetland, 1.1 acres of freshwater wetland along the railroad berm, and the rest upland, paths, a disc-golf course, the Dreamland playground, the BPRC boathouse and Waterside Workshops. **\[High\]** (APIP 2008).

**Historic status.** Aquatic Park is not a City of Berkeley Landmark and is not on the National Register. The City's January 2023 landmarks list has no entry for it; the 1999 Historic Property Survey Report for the I-80 bike bridge, cited in the APIP bibliography, is the likeliest eligibility document and was not located online. **\[High\]** on absence from the City list; **\[Unknown\]** on eligibility.

**Open question.** The brief for this page assumed a 1992–93 Caltrans widening of I-80 along the park. No document found ties a 1990s widening to the tubes; the documented changes are the 1954–60 freeway conversion, a later widening that the Living New Deal says "took out another row of plantings," and the 2015–18 barrier project. Treat 1992–93 as unverified. **\[Low\]**

## D. The weir at 2nd & University: why it was built and how it works

Strawberry Creek, the City's largest watershed, reaches the Bay in a concrete box culvert under University Avenue. The lagoon lies a block to the south of that culvert and below it. The weir is the thing that keeps the creek out of the lagoon most of the time.

### What the public record says

The 2008 APIP study inventories every connection to the lagoons. For Strawberry it lists a 90-inch diameter concrete storm drain in "ok" condition and a 90-inch cement connection pipe to the main lagoon whose lagoon-side outlet is "partially buried." It states the rule: "There is an overflow connection at the Strawberry Stormdrain to the Main Lagoon. A weir in the connecting pipe prevents tidal flows from entering the Main Lagoon. Only large rainfall events generate sufficient storm flows to overtop the weir," and gives the threshold: the overflow pipe "only carries stormwater to the Main Lagoon when flows reach about 5.5 ft. in the pipe." Its Figure 94 is a drawing and photograph of the weir, "looking upstream from the Main Lagoon Connection." The study also lists a separate "Strawberry 12-inch low-flow drain connection" that its model shows carrying nothing. **\[High\]**

The City's April 2022 fact sheet names the structure: "the old 12-inch high redwood weir in the University Avenue storm drain." Its stated purpose is to keep the Bay out: with the board gone, the overflow let "water from the Bay enter Aquatic Park during high tides." The fact sheet adds that "such an overflow is normal during higher tides and storm conditions," so the City treats some tidal spill as expected. **\[High\]**

**The 1990 and 2012 plans.** Philip Williams & Associates' 1990 hydrology report for the Master Plan describes the Potter drain as a horseshoe section 9 feet wide and 7 feet high with a 96-inch-wide box outfall; it is the Potter drain, not Strawberry, that the record sizes at roughly 7 by 9 feet. The 2012 Watershed Management Plan modeled Potter and Codornices only, treats the lagoon as a receiving water for up to 68 acre-feet of a 10-year Potter storm, prices a Potter pressure pipe to the Bay as the watershed's first priority, and does not mention the Strawberry weir or the tide tubes; its only Strawberry item near the park is the "University Outfall (behind Seabreeze Market)" as an illicit-discharge screening point. **\[High\]**

The study's watershed model gives the flows the weir is managing. The Strawberry drain collects 2,022 acres; the Potter drain 1,995 acres. Peak flows at the park, in cubic feet per second: **\[High\]**

| Design storm (24-hour) | Strawberry peak, cfs | Potter peak, cfs | Strawberry volume, acre-feet |
|----|----|----|----|
| First flush (0.3-year, 1.0 inch) | 212 | 358 | 84 |
| 5-year | 883 | 1,396 | 395 |
| 10-year | 1,040 | 1,619 | 469 |
| 25-year | 1,299 | 1,978 | 592 |
| 100-year | 1,664 | 2,477 | 768 |

APIP 2008, Table 16. The whole main lagoon holds roughly 22 acre-feet of tidal exchange per cycle under existing conditions, so a single 5-year Strawberry storm carries about eighteen times that volume past the weir.

**The design intent, in the City's own words.** The APIP treats the weir as a problem to remove, not a feature to keep: Alternative 4B would take the weir out so the connection sits "at an elevation that will allow tidal flow to enter and leave the lagoon," then install slide gates to "block stormwater inflow volumes at new connections," especially the fall first flush. In other words the City's plan since 2008 has been to swap a passive weir, which blocks tides and lets in big storms, for an active gate, which would admit tides and block storms. That plan has not been built. **\[High\]**

### What Steve's files add

Three documents in Steve Kromer's 2019–2021 working folder describe the structure itself. None is published; each is cited here as a source Steve holds.

- **Field notes by Jim McGrath, October and November 2019.** The Strawberry culvert is "a very large concrete box culvert, 7 feet by 9 feet" (the published record gives those dimensions for the Potter drain and lists Strawberry at this point as a 90-inch pipe; which is right at 2nd & University is an open question). The APIP design documents "a 15 foot-long weir that allows very large flows to spill over the weir and into a secondary drain into Aquatic Park," and "that weir features a 1 foot high wooden flashboard that maintains a weir height above most king tides. Normally, it would be impossible for flow during the dry season to enter the lagoon at all." The notes record Public Works' August 1–2, 2019 measurement of a 0.9-foot rise in the lagoon, which McGrath converts to about 40 acre-feet, "about 4 times the estimated inflow in APIP for the tide tubes if they were functioning." **\[Moderate\]** on the weir and board, which the City's 2022 fact sheet confirms ("12-inch high redwood"); **\[Low\]** on the 7-by-9 culvert and the 15-foot length, which no public document gives for Strawberry.
- **City CCTV inspection report, January 28, 2021.** A one-page POSM report for manhole 068-900 on University Avenue at the 2nd Street overpass. Two stills from a 2009 camera run show a concrete shelf running the length of the chamber with a stack of white-painted wooden boards bolted to steel posts along its crest, one board marked "W" with an arrow and "TOP." The inspector logs "Shelf Status: Serviceable" and "Channel Status: Serviceable," severity none. This is the weir with a board in place. **\[High\]** that the City inspected and found it serviceable on that date. Two cautions: the stills carry a "1/1/2009" camera timestamp, and by January 2021 the board should have been the metal plate installed in December 2019, so whether the pictures show the new weir, an unset camera clock, or an archived 2009 run is an open question.
- **Meeting notes, November 7, 2019.** A City engineer tells stakeholders "we're gonna go in to check weir," "in a week or two," and that the tubes will be cameraed "in a few weeks." The same notes record staff describing the east-side separators as "designed to flow into the lagoon," a stakeholder reading the 2012 EIR as sending large flows to Potter, and staff replying "not sure if that's correct." **\[High\]** as a record of what was said.

*\[Diagram on the web page.\]* **How the weir works.** A cross-section looking west along the Strawberry Creek culvert at 2nd & University. In dry weather the creek runs along the floor; an ordinary high tide pushing in from the Bay raises the water in the culvert but not to the board, while a king tide raised by a storm surge to about 8.6 feet reached over it (observed November 24, 2026); a large storm fills the culvert past the board and spills into the overflow chamber and pipe to the lagoon. With the board gone the crest is a foot lower, so smaller storms and higher tides spill. The board height is from the City's 2022 fact sheet (12 inches); the overflow threshold from the APIP (about 5.5 ft of depth in the pipe); the box shape and the 7-by-9 dimension from McGrath's notes, which the published record assigns to the Potter drain. The crest elevation, the weir length and the overflow pipe size are not in any document consulted.

*\[Diagram on the web page.\]* **Every way water gets in or out.** Bay water enters only through the five tide tubes under I-80 at mid-lagoon, plus one small tube to the Model Yacht Basin. Storm water, and Bay water at surge-raised high tides, enters from the Strawberry drain (over the weir, north end), the Potter drain (tidal, through two 24-inch culverts set about 3 feet above the pipe floor, south end) and the east-side street outfalls. Schematic, after APIP 2008 Figures 12 and 18.

**Try it: [the interactive water model](aquatic-park-water-model.html).** An animated water balance of the lagoon. Change the tide tubes' condition, take the weir board in or out, pick a tide and a design storm, and watch the level, salinity and storm water respond. Its three fitted constants reproduce the APIP's 22 and 115 acre-feet per tide and its 48.5- and 10.4-day flushing times; its tide settings include the predicted November 2026 king tide and the surge that raised it about 1.35 feet. It is an explainer, not an engineering model.

### What the November 2026 king tides showed

**A surge-raised king tide topped the weir with the board in.** On the morning of November 24, 2026, observers watched Bay water flowing into the lagoon's north end from the Strawberry box culvert, and both Jim McGrath and Susan Schwartz of Friends of Five Creeks judged the board to be in place. The predicted high water was 7.34 feet above MLLW, but a storm surge put the actual Bay about 1.35 feet higher, near 8.6 feet. So the observation shows only that the top of the board sits below about 8.6 feet. It does not show whether an ordinary king tide of around 7.3 feet, without a surge, would top it; ordinary higher-high tides (6.1 feet on average) almost certainly do not. That fits both McGrath's 2019 description of a board set "above most king tides" and the City's 2022 fact sheet, which calls overflow "normal during higher tides and storm conditions." The weir is a filter on water height: it keeps out the daily tide and lets in the rare surge-driven extremes, and with them whatever is sitting in the lower Strawberry culvert. Since surges come with storms, that is also when storm runoff is in the culvert. **\[High\]** on the tide heights (NOAA gauges); **\[Moderate\]** on the board being in place, which no one inspected.

**The lagoon drained fast after the November 17 storm.** About 2.5 inches of rain fell on November 17. A week later the lagoon stood about two feet below the storm's mud line on the rushes, which McGrath notes is roughly the lagoon's whole tidal range, despite two days of king-tide inflow at the north end. His reading: the tide tubes cleared in 2020–2022 are still largely open, and the repair did what it was meant to do for flood control. He recalls the APIP estimating up to 31 days for the lagoon to drain after heavy rain; the APIP figure this page cites is 48.5 days after a 100-year storm. **\[Moderate\]**

**The counterargument.** A mud line on rushes is a rough gauge, the drop could include evaporation and seepage, and a 2.5-inch storm is far smaller than the 100-year event the APIP's 48.5 days describes, so one week's recovery does not prove the tubes are near design capacity. The City's 2023 evaluation still expects the clay sections to fail by about 2030. The test McGrath proposes is the right one: compare the City's measured lagoon levels for November 17, 23 and 24 with the Bay tide for the same hours. Those records are on the request list below. **\[Unknown\]** until they are produced.

### Why it matters: three failure modes in one structure

**1. The board goes missing.** Then the crest is a foot lower. High tides back up the storm drain from the Bay and spill over the crest into the lagoon twice a day, and storms far smaller than designed spill too. That is what the City says happened in 2019: a ten-inch rise with no rain, water filmed entering at the Addison outfall at high tide, and a redwood board that "had disappeared." The Water Board's version is slightly different, "additional discharges into the lagoon from the storm sewer"; the two are compatible, since whatever was in the culvert at high tide came over the crest. The alternatives McGrath listed in October, a partly blocked outlet, construction dewatering discharged to the storm drain, or damage from nearby construction, were overtaken by the finding; the City told the Water Board its sewer and storm-drain investigation found no evidence of those as bacteria sources. **\[High\]** on the City's and Board's accounts; **\[Moderate\]** on the mechanism as drawn above, which is inferred from them.

**2. The board stays and the tide tubes fail.** Then storm water that does spill in has nowhere to go: the APIP computed 48.5 days to clear a 100-year storm's water from the lagoons under existing conditions, against 10.4 days with the recommended improvements. That is the 2017 flooding at Waterside Workshops, and the 2023 salinity crash. **\[High\]** (APIP; Berkeleyside, Feb 2017; City Manager memo, Feb 2023).

**3. The board does its job and nobody looks at it.** McGrath's point in 2019 was institutional: the weir sits on the Public Works storm-drain system, the lagoon belongs to Parks, the tubes pass under a Caltrans freeway, and Environmental Health posts the signs. "When many people share responsibility, no one is responsible." The City's own 2008 study had asked for control structures and an operations plan; the October 2020 presentation lists an "O&M plan for main tide tubes" as still being scoped. No published maintenance schedule for the weir or the tubes was found, and the City's first attribution of the 2019 rise was to the tubes, not the weir. **\[Moderate\]** (absence of evidence).

**The counterargument.** A flashboard weir is cheap, passive, and exactly what a 1930s or 1970s engineer would specify for a connection that should almost never operate; a missing board is a maintenance event, and the City found it and later recorded the shelf serviceable on camera. The CCTV report is evidence of a working inspection program, not of neglect. And the lagoon is a stormwater receiving body by design: the 2008 study's own alternative keeps a storm connection and adds gates, which means more operating burden, not less. The open question is not whether the weir should exist but whether anyone is assigned to look at it before the first rain each year.

## E. Water quality, sediment and the tide tubes

### The 2019 contamination, in the City's numbers

The City's Environmental Health division sampled three stations (north, middle, south). The spring and summer 2019 samples went to the Alameda County Public Health Lab; after the August rise the north station read above 1,600 and 2,400 (diluted) on August 20 and 26. The County's columns are not labeled by constituent in the copy Steve holds, so those figures are reported as the table gives them. From September 17, 2019 a contract lab, Alpha Analytical, tested for Enterococcus, the only indicator the State standard for the lagoon uses: 370, 650 and 520 cfu/100 mL at the three stations on September 17; 370, 550 and 690 on September 20; above 2,419, 390 and 1,700 on September 23. The State single-sample standard is 110. **\[High\]** (City results tables, copies in Steve's files).

At the November 7, 2019 meeting staff said the earlier testing had not used "the right bacteria," that the City now had a consultant (Wood Environmental) and would "keep testing," and that it could not say whether the source was human or animal. The question of sampling the sediment, which stakeholders raised because disturbed sediment can release dormant Enterococcus, was answered "we don't test the sediment." The City Manager's October 18, 2019 memo listed possible sources; McGrath's reply argued it ignored the most direct evidence, the inflow at the Strawberry overflow. **\[High\]** as a record of what was said; the City's memo itself was not retrieved. **\[Unknown\]**

### Sixteen months of weekly sampling, October 30, 2019 – February 2, 2021

The City's own results table, in the February 11, 2021 work-group copy, holds 47 sampling dates. Only Enterococcus governs posting. Counting every reading above the 110 standard: **\[High\]**

|----|----|----|----|
| North (Addison end, nearest the Strawberry overflow) | 47 | 8 | **1,300** (Dec 15, 2020, two days after "fairly significant rainfall") |
| Middle | 47 | 7 | **1,900** (Dec 23, 2019) |
| South (Model Yacht Basin end, nearest Potter) | 46 | 7 | **430** (Dec 30, 2019) |

Non-detects counted as zero; "\<10" counted as 5. No samples were collected from April 23 to September 16, 2020; the table does not say why. The table's own comments column ties the December 15, 2020, January 5, 2021 and February 2, 2021 exceedances to rain on the preceding days and notes "no reported sewage releases" each time; the January 19, 2021 south-station reading of 200 is annotated to strong winds with no rain.

**What the pattern says.** In dry weather the lagoon met the standard at every station from late February 2020 on, with two exceptions. After rain it did not. That is the signature of storm water carrying bacteria into a basin that cannot flush, not of a single point source; it is also consistent with the APIP's 2008 finding that first-flush runoff is the main contaminant load and that the lagoon's residence time is the problem. It does not identify where the bacteria come from, and no source-tracking result (human versus animal DNA markers) was ever published. **\[Moderate\]**

**And since.** The City has kept sampling weekly and posts the table ("Aquatic Park Lagoon Enterococcus Sampling Results") on its website. From September 2020 through October 22, 2026 that table holds about 280 sampling dates; at least one station exceeded 110 on roughly one in five of them, almost always on or after a rain, with single readings as high as 24,196 (November 13, 2025) and 15,000 (October 10, 2024). The yellow advisory was in effect on October 22, 2026, the latest date in the table. **\[Moderate\]** (counted from a text extraction of the City's posted table; a few dates in the extraction are mis-typed and the count is approximate).

**The counterargument.** Fourteen exceedances in 47 weeks, most of them wet-weather, is what an urban lagoon in a 4,000-acre catchment looks like, and the standard is written for ocean beaches. The City closed the lagoon to contact when it had to, tested weekly for sixteen months, and published the numbers to the stakeholder group. By that measure the response worked. The reply is in the APIP: the City knew in 2004 and 2008 that the lagoon could not flush and chose, in 2012–2014, not to build the fix.

### Sediment and the tide tubes

**The tubes.** Five 24-inch pipes under I-80 are the main lagoon's only Bay connection. The 2008 study found their Bay-side ends "falling apart," occluded by failing riprap and broken pipe; the Model Yacht Basin tube buried in sand; the Radio Tower Pond tube collapsed under the frontage road. McGrath's October 2019 visit, timed to a high tide, found "only a tiny amount of flow through one or two of the tubes," bedded sediment on the lagoon side of three of them "so long there is algae on the surface," and the slide gates "inoperable." The City's 2020 project cleared all five of tubeworm, an invasive serpulid first seen on the West Coast at the Berkeley Marina in the 1920s that prefers brackish water and clogs pipes. Its October 2020 slides show the "before" outlets choked with rock and the "after" outlets open. **\[High\]**

**What the camera found.** The 2020 CCTV run fed a condition assessment (Harris & Associates) whose finding the 2023 Council item reports: "a large portion of the five main Tide Tubes are clay walls that are failing," replace or sleeve them, expect "complete failure in no later than 10 years from 2020." Three other tubes (two lagoon-to-basin, one basin-to-Bay) are "significantly clogged." The Harris & Associates memo of November 11, 2020 settles the material: 150 feet of 1930s vitrified clay pipe from the lagoon, then 100 feet of 1950s concrete pipe to the Bay, the clay "close to collapse" at all five tubes. The APIP had called them cement; it was describing the Bay-side ends. The City's 2022 fact sheet puts the tubes' operating efficiency at "less than 10%" in 2019 against 60% in 2007. **\[High\]**

**How much flows out at low tide: a first measurement, November 24, 2026.** At 4:16 pm, with the Bay at about 0.5 feet above MLLW and falling, Steve Kromer filmed the tide-tube outlets on the Bay side and photographed the staff gauge at the five slide gates on the lagoon side. The outlets are buried in riprap; the water comes out between the rocks as a hard white jet and runs across bare sand to the Bay, so the tubes were discharging freely above the Bay surface. The gauge read 6.0 feet. Its datum is not marked; MLLW or NAVD88 differ by only 0.13 feet here, so the lagoon stood at about 5.9 to 6.0 feet MLLW, some 5.5 feet above the Bay. **\[High\]** on the readings and timing (photo and video metadata); **\[Moderate\]** on the datum.

Five 24-inch pipes about 250 feet long, running clear under a 5.5-foot head, would carry about 125 to 130 cubic feet per second, some 25 cfs per tube at 7 to 9 feet per second. But a water balance driven by the actual surge-raised Bay record can hold the lagoon at only about 5.3 feet at that hour if the tubes run clear. Something kept the lagoon 0.6 to 0.7 feet higher, and only two things can. **Either** about 20 cfs of extra water was coming in (dry-weather flow from the Strawberry low-flow pipe and the east-side drains, or leftover runoff from the November 17 storm), and the tubes carried about 125 to 130 cfs; **or** the outflow was being held back at the lagoon-side gates (slide gates partly lowered, or a high inlet sill), and the tubes carried only about 40 to 55 cfs. Foam tracked in the video gives a surface speed of 2 to 3 feet per second on the beach and a rough 20 to 80 cfs, which leans toward the second case but cannot decide it. **\[Moderate\]** that the flow lay between 40 and 130 cfs (about 26 to 84 million gallons a day); **\[Low\]** on which case applies.

**How to settle it.** Two gauge readings half an hour to an hour apart on a falling tide measure the outflow directly: the main lagoon covers about 58 acres, so each inch it falls per hour is about 59 cfs leaving through the tubes and the Potter path together. The two cases predict a fall of about 2.3 inches an hour (clear tubes, extra inflow) against about 1.4 inches an hour (restricted at the gates). A photo of the slide-gate positions at the same time would explain whichever it is.

**Sediment.** Stakeholders put accumulation at "1 inch/year" in 2019; the 2020 project removed about 1,300 cubic yards from the tube inlet area, dried it on site, and needed roughly \$400,000 it did not then have to haul it away; the haul-off was done in November 2022. The APIP gives no annual rate but records the main lagoon's measured high-tide level falling from -1.57 ft in 2002 to -1.95 ft in 2007; the June 25, 2024 EPA grant item says the lagoon's average depth has "decreased by approximately 50% since 1970." The 2020 presentation lists a bathymetric survey as being scoped; no result was found. **\[Moderate\]** on the rate (a stakeholder estimate); **\[High\]** on the volumes and the City statements.

**The photographs in Steve's folder**, described rather than reproduced. A November 6, 2019 photograph, geotagged on the lagoon's west shore, is a stitched panorama inside the five-tube inlet box: a rectangular concrete chamber under a steel grate, green water with foam at the surface, stained walls, a single marked "Functional Inlet" at the lower right. An August 10, 2018 drone mosaic shows the whole lagoon from above, the water olive-green and the south half mottled with pale submerged growth. A low-resolution map, used for the SAPAC walk, is the City's APIP storm-drain figure with ten numbered stops drawn on it. One further image in the folder is a personal photograph unrelated to the park and is not described.

**What the tubes cannot do.** Even repaired, five 24-inch pipes move about 22 acre-feet a tidal cycle into a 58-acre lagoon; the APIP's target of 115 acre-feet needs the larger connections at Strawberry and Potter. The State transportation grant replaces the tubes under the freeway because the scour threatens I-80; it does not fund the circulation plan. **\[High\]**

## F. Who pays

### Which fund the park lives in

Aquatic Park is a Parks-division park, not a Waterfront or Marina asset. The City's budget narrative describes the Marina Fund (608) as covering the Marina plus "Waterfront parks and facilities including Cesar Chavez Park, Shorebird Park and Nature Center, and the Adventure Playground"; Aquatic Park is not on that list. After Measure Y the Parks Department's recurring capital is about \$3.6M a year: Parks Tax \$2.75M, Capital Improvement Fund \$400,000, Marina Fund \$350,000, Camps \$100,000; minor maintenance is \$675,000 more. Every Aquatic Park project competes inside that envelope or finds a grant. **\[High\]** (FY2027–31 proposed CIP).

- **What is happening at the end of Bancroft?** Construction is under way just east of the railroad where the EPA grant scoped a second trash separator, besides Channing; Friends of Five Creeks has asked the City what it is and has no answer.
