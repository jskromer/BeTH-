The CIP Studio and the Dashboard Referral, Compared with Vision 2050

Vision 2050 has already committed Berkeley, on paper, to multi-criteria prioritization, cost and schedule tracking, a public website, outcome indicators and semi-annual performance reports. This page sets those commitments next to what the UC Berkeley CIP studio proposed in February 2026 and what the Council referred to the City Manager on September 29, 2026.

CWhat the comparison shows

1. The referral repeats a 2022 commitment, with one important change

The 2022 Program Plan already said performance reports would go to Council semi-annually and be posted publicly. It made that machinery conditional: the program team, the Program Management Manual and the oversight committee would come "if voters approve funding." Measure L, the $650M bond, failed in November 2022 (59.42% yes against a two-thirds requirement), and no semi-annual Vision 2050 performance report has appeared in the public record since. The September 2026 referral asks for semi-annual reports again, but this time with no condition on a revenue measure. That decoupling is the substantive change. High on the texts; Moderate that no semi-annual Vision 2050 report has been published, since this is an absence in the public record.

2. Everyone tracks inputs; Vision 2050 asked for outcomes

The 2022 plan's 26 KPIs measure results: pavement condition, sidewalk safety, injuries, retrofits, canopy. The studio's schema stores budget, years and two yes/no flags. The referral asks for "schedules and their budgets." Neither connects a project to the indicator it is supposed to move. A tracker built to the referral's wording would show whether money was spent on time. It would not show whether streets got better. High on the content of each; the reading is ours.

3. Three scoring systems are now in play, plus a fourth proposal

Vision 2050's 60/40 Envision/community scorecard (2022, by asset category); the six-factor rubric used to build the Measure U project list (May 2026: health, life and safety 20; condition, accessibility and resilience 20; community use and equity 20; green resiliency and sustainability 20; deliverability and readiness 10; external funding 10); and the Budget Referral tiers and Public Works priority labels in the CIP. The studio proposes a fourth, without weights, and recommends against "readiness," which the Measure U rubric weights at 10. The referral does not say which one the Program Plan update should use. High on each system as published.

4. The studio did not use the Vision 2050 documents that bear most on its subject

Its only Vision 2050 sources are the 2020 Framework and the December 2025 TF packet. It does not cite the 2022 Program Plan's tracking tools, KPI table or semi-annual reporting, nor the 2022 SAMP or NexGen. It treats the 60/40 scorecard as a project-ranking tool. The 2022 plan says plainly that the scorecard ranked asset categories and that project ranking would come later. High

5. Vision 2050's own milestones are the referral's first test

The TF set a Program Plan update for Spring 2026, an AMP steering committee and the start of condition assessment for July 2026, and an AMP for four asset classes by July 2027. The referral's first semi-annual report falls around late March 2027. If it does not say where those four milestones stand, the reporting cycle is not tracking Vision 2050. High on the dates; Moderate on the report date (six months from adoption; no date is set).

DThe counterargument

Strongest case for the referral as written

Vision 2050 is a long-range framework whose project machinery was designed to switch on with new funding. Without that funding, the honest course is to restart from the CIP the City actually adopts, and that is what the referral does. Tracking spending and schedule comes first because outcome indicators mean little until project data exists. The studio produced a working tracker template in one semester; the 2022 plan's website is now offline. Asking for less and getting it beats re-adopting 26 KPIs no one reports. Moderate

Assessment

The sequencing argument is right: a project ledger has to exist before outcomes can be attached to it. But the referral's wording would lock in a schedule-and-budget tracker as the definition of done. The KPI table and the targets are already adopted. Adding two fields to each project record would connect the dashboard to Vision 2050 rather than run parallel to it: the Table 9 indicator the project serves, and the baseline condition it addresses. That costs a column. Deferring it turns the "transparency hub" into a spending ledger with a map. Moderate; this is a judgment, not a finding.

EQuestions for the first semi-annual report

  • Which scoring system will the updated Program Plan use: the 2022 60/40 scorecard, the Measure U six-factor rubric, or something new? With what weights?
  • Will the dashboard carry, for each project, the Table 9 KPI it serves and a baseline condition value?
  • Where do the TF's July 2026 milestones stand: AMP steering committee, condition assessment start?
  • Will NexGen condition data be published by asset class?
  • Will the dashboard be built from the CIP's structured data (project codes, fund splits, department priority), or re-keyed from the PDF as the prototype was?
  • What became of BerkeleyVision2050.org, and will the dashboard replace it?

FThe studio, the referral, the people, and the budget

The studio and the referral came out of the same Mayor's-office infrastructure effort. Who ran it, what each document does well and badly, and where the referral sits against the budget.

Who is involved

  • Matt Nichols. Listed under "Project sponsors" in the Realize Vision 2050 Task Force roster as "Mayor's advisor on infrastructure," alongside Mayor Ishii, Councilmember Taplin and Public Works Director Terrance Davis. The studio report acknowledges him as its client contact in the Mayor's Office. Principal Transportation Planner for Berkeley from 2002 to 2015, then Director of Infrastructure & Transportation for Oakland Mayor Libby Schaaf, where he worked on Oakland's infrastructure bond. He wrote the October 8, 2026 Berkeleyside op-ed "Vote Yes on Measure U"; its author note gives his Berkeley and Oakland history and does not mention his current advisory role to the Mayor. He is not listed on the Mayor's published staff page, and his employment status there is not stated in any document reviewed. High on each listed fact; Unknown on employment status.
  • Cydney Chilimidos. Senior Policy Analyst in the Mayor's office, portfolio "Public Safety and Infrastructure," per the Mayor's team page. Her published background is in law, with a focus on disability rights. The referral names only the Mayor as its contact, so her role in drafting it is not on the record. High on the title and portfolio; Moderate that the referral was staffed from this portfolio.
  • What it adds up to. One small team sponsored the Task Force, acted as client for the studio, wrote the referral, and is campaigning for the bond. That gives the work continuity. It also means the people asking for the dashboard are the same people whose program it would measure. High on the roles; Moderate on the reading.

Strengths and weaknesses

UC studio (February 2026)Mayor's referral (September 29, 2026)
Strengths Real peer review: about 45 sources and six interviews. An explicit scoring structure (eight impact metrics plus a 0–4 urgency score). Candid about its own limits: no weights, an unbuilt data layer, upkeep not costed. The Ann Arbor interview gives a staffing benchmark: one person maintains a 400-plus project tracker. The prototype exists and can be queried by anyone. Authority: adopted unanimously on consent with three co-sponsors. It binds the City Manager to report to Council every six months. It drops the 2022 plan's condition tying reporting to a new revenue measure. It asks for the Program Plan and the asset management plan together, not only a map.
Weaknesses Hand-copied data from the CIP PDF; on-time and on-budget flags with no stated source; 6 of 16 timelines that do not match the years the CIP funds. It does not cite the 2022 Program Plan or its 26 outcome indicators. It is hosted on a student account that nobody is charged with maintaining. It cites a "Test Template" as its model without setting a data standard for the City's version. It names no scoring method, no outcome indicators and no targets. The $200,000 is the sponsor's estimate, covers the two plans, and does not cover the dashboard. Its $1.5 billion need figure is below both recent CIP figures ($1.65 billion in FY2025–29; $2.1 billion cited for the proposed FY2027–31). No staff owner is named.
Answers to A professor and a semester deadline The City Manager, through a referral with no deadline and no appropriation

Assessment: the studio did the analysis and the referral has the authority. But the referral adopted the studio's thinnest feature, a schedule-and-budget tracker, and left out its most useful finding: Ann Arbor's tracker works because a named person owns the data. Moderate; this is a judgment.

Where the referral meets the budget

  • It came after the budget and appropriates nothing. The FY2027–28 biennial budget was adopted in June 2026. A referral adopted on consent sends work to the City Manager; it does not move money. Our September 29 scan lists the $200,000 separately from spending as the sponsor's estimate. A referral with a cost normally waits for a budget cycle (a mid-year amendment or the next budget), where a high rank in the referral queue still does not guarantee funding. High that nothing was appropriated; Moderate on which cycle it reaches.
  • It arrives in a deficit year. FY2027 opened with a $29.2 million structural General Fund deficit and 138 proposed layoffs (deficit ledger). The ongoing data owner a tracker needs is the kind of new position a layoff budget does not add, and the referral does not ask for one. High on the deficit; Moderate on the consequence.
  • Measure U is the obvious funding route, but the referral does not say so. Of Measure U's roughly $313 million, about $40.5 million is set aside for "staffing and implementation resources," including "reporting, auditing, and oversight." That could cover the plans and a tracker without General Fund money. It would also bring back the 2022 arrangement, in which the reporting machinery depends on a bond passing. High on Measure U's terms; Moderate on the link, which no document makes.
  • The tracker is a budget-data product. Every field a working tracker needs is already in the CIP, which is part of the budget book: project code, fund split, budget by phase, department priority. Built from the City's financial and Public Works systems, the dashboard is cheap. Re-typed from a PDF, as the prototype was, it is not. High that the fields exist (data review, section D).
  • An example a tracker would settle. The South Berkeley Senior Center appears in the prototype as a $3,000,000 project funded by Measure T1 in FY2024–25. Measure U's project list, as summarized in the October 8 op-ed, funds HVAC repair and seismic upgrades there again. That may be different scope. Without project-level tracking, a voter cannot tell. High on both listings; Moderate that they overlap.

Questions for the City Manager: Which budget cycle carries the $200,000? Who owns the dashboard data, and is that position funded? If Measure U passes, does part of its implementation set-aside fund the plans and the tracker? If it fails, does the referral still go forward?
