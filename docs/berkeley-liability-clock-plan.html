§1 — The premise

A clock is only honest if it's a derivative

The National Debt Clock works because there is a real accrual underneath it. Ours has to work the same way, or it's a stunt that a reporter kills in one phone call.

The whole product rests on one commitment: every tick traces to a formula and a sourced row. Not a number that goes up because going-up is dramatic — a number that goes up because deferral genuinely compounds, at a rate we can show our work on.

That commitment is also the argument. If the clock is reproducible, then the conclusion it leads to — that we can optimize these decisions, and are currently choosing not to — arrives as arithmetic rather than as opinion. That is the entire reason to build this instead of writing another op-ed.

The reproducibility test. A "download the model" button and a Python file that regenerates every published figure. If a skeptic can run python3 model/build.py and get our number to the dollar, we win the argument before it starts. If they can't, the clock is a liability of its own.

§2 — The engine

Four accruals, four formulas

The ticker rate is the sum of four independently-computed components. Each is switchable, each carries its own confidence flag, and the UI can show the clock with any subset turned on.

ComponentWhat it measuresFormulaData status
Escalation Fixed backlog scope, rising construction costs backlog × ENR CCI have it
Deterioration Assets decaying past the cheap-fix window. The $1 seal that becomes a $7 reconstruction. Σ lane-mi × Δcost/PCI band needs StreetSaver
Under-investment gap Annual steady-state need minus annual funding, accruing daily (need − funded) / 365 needs per-class need
Actuarial Interest on the unfunded pension and retiree-health liability UAL × (disc − contrib) have series, need params

Do not merge the two liabilities into one number

Pension/OPEB and deferred capital are different kinds of obligation. One is a legal debt to people; the other is a physical debt to assets, and you can technically choose not to pay it — you just pay more later. Merging them into a single headline is the fastest way to lose a finance-literate critic.

Design answer: the hero is a stack of two tickers, not one. They sit adjacent, sized against each other, with a toggle that combines them and a line of type that says exactly what combining them means. Making the seam visible is more persuasive than hiding it.

§3 — Architecture

Python engine, JSON contract, static client

You asked about Streamlit. Short answer: yes — but as your internal lab, not as the thing voters open.

Streamlit reruns its whole script on every interaction, which is exactly wrong for a counter that must animate at 60fps. It needs a live server, so a link from a mailer or a QR code at a door hits a 10–30 second cold start, and a spike of traffic on election week takes it down. And it can't be embedded in d1-site or the BeTH docs, where your entire existing corpus is self-contained HTML.

Where Streamlit genuinely earns its place is upstream: calibrating the deterioration curve, running sensitivity sweeps and Monte Carlo on the ranges, and sitting in front of a civil engineer or an actuary while you sanity-check assumptions before anything goes public. Build it. Keep it in lab/. Never ship it as the product.

You already have this exact pattern working in event-value-twin/: a stdlib-only Python model, a seed JSON, and a self-contained dashboard whose every figure the model reproduces. Clone the shape.

liability-clock/
  data/
    assets.json        # asset classes: scope, condition, need, funding, mandate, confidence
    liabilities.json   # pension + OPEB series and actuarial params
    budget.json        # from d1-site/data/budget-spine.json (all-funds by dept FY24–28)
    revenues.json      # measures, rates, yields, sunset dates
    scenarios.json     # named policy packages
    sources.json       # one id per citation; every number points at one
  model/
    clock.py           # the four accruals -> $/sec
    tco.py             # 30-year lifecycle simulator
    optimize.py        # rubric-ranked allocation under a budget constraint
    build.py           # emits public/model.json + golden-file test
  lab/
    app.py             # Streamlit: calibration, sensitivity, Monte Carlo
  public/
    index.html         # the clock, the drill-down, the overlay
    model.json

Determinism rule. The browser computes nothing the Python doesn't also compute. The client interpolates the clock between ticks and renders scenarios from precomputed coefficients; it never invents a number. Enforce it with a golden-file test in CI.

The field that makes the whole tool work

Mandate level. Sewer sits under a federal consent decree. Stormwater sits under a regional permit. Sidewalks are partly ADA. Seismic is life-safety. Once you render that column honestly, a fact lands that no op-ed can deliver: most of this bill is not optional, and the discretionary part is mostly streets and parks — the things voters actually see.

That single realization reframes the argument from "the City wastes money" to "the City has less room than you think, which is precisely why the remaining room has to be spent well." It's the pivot from blame to optimization, and it comes out of a data field rather than out of rhetoric.

Per-class fields to add on top of what the workbook already has: escalation_rate (class-specific — ENR for concrete, medical trend for OPEB), deferral_curve params, annual_need_steady_state, funding streams with sunset dates, confidence, and source_ids.

§5 — The exemplar

Build streets all the way to the bottom, then clone

Do not build ten shallow drill-downs. Build one that goes to the floor, prove the pattern, then replicate. Streets is the right one: best data, most legible, and the deferral math is starkest there.

Condition

  • PCI gauge with MTC tier bands
  • Berkeley at 56, in "At Risk" (50–59)
  • Peer cities on the same axis
  • Trend line, not just a point

The deferral curve

  • Cost per lane-mile by PCI band
  • Slider: "wait N more years"
  • Watch the backlog grow as you wait
  • This is the teaching object

The funding stack

  • Measure FF 45% ≈ $6.75M
  • Measure BB ≈ $2.98M
  • SB1 / gas tax ≈ $2.35M
  • CIP + GF → ~$18M/yr total

The line that does the most work on the whole site. Berkeley's own adopted 5-Year Street Rehabilitation Plan states that its funding level does not reach "good." Council adopted a plan that, on its face, keeps the streets at "At Risk" — and then the ballot arguments describe the same streets as a crisis. Put the adopted plan's own sentence on screen, cited, next to the two target lines: what holds PCI at 56 versus what reaches 70, each with a price.

Those two target figures are the one thing here you cannot fake, and they are the reason Phase 0 needs the StreetSaver/MTC pavement model rather than a plausible-looking curve. Get them from the City's own model or request them; do not estimate them and publish.

§6 — The overlay

Every lever is a decision a councilmember actually votes on

This is where the tool stops being alarming and starts being instructional. The rule that keeps it credible: no abstract sliders. Each control is a real, currently-available Berkeley choice, in the units it appears on an agenda.

Capital levers

  • Annual paving $
  • Sidewalk program $
  • Facilities / seismic $
  • Marina: invest · divest · drift

Revenue levers

  • $300M GO bond — pass / fail
  • Sales & use tax — pass / fail
  • Stormwater fee update (Prop 218)
  • Measure sunsets and renewals

Operating levers

  • FTE trajectory (1,604.84 base)
  • Health cost-sharing
  • Carryover discipline
  • Adopt the GFOA rubric — y/n

Three panels, always visible, always at once

PanelShowsWhy it's there
Annual cashWhat you pay each year, 30-year horizonThis is the only thing today's budget process optimizes
Total cost of ownershipCumulative spend incl. deferral penalty and debt serviceThe number nobody votes on
Condition / servicePCI, hazards cleared, seismic status, sewer complianceWhat the money actually bought

The lesson has to be discovered, not stated. Sliders that lower panel 1 in years 1–5 raise panel 2 by more. Nobody should have to be told that — they should find it by playing, three or four times, and then read one sentence confirming what they just saw. Add a persistent readout: asset-life purchased per dollar spent.

Do the math on your own side first

A debt-service module, applied to the bond whatever your position on it: $300M at ~5% over 30 years is roughly $19.5M/yr, about $585M all-in, of which $285M is interest. Show that plainly, in the same type as everything else. Running the honest arithmetic on the measure you might personally support is what buys the credibility to run it on anything else.

§7 — The punchline

The optimizer is the argument

Presets let people compare: status quo · both measures pass · both fail · bond only · tax only · fix-it-first. Then one more, which is the point of the entire build.

"Optimized" runs your already-drafted GFOA-aligned rubric — legal mandate 20, health & safety 18, condition 15, cost of deferral 12, service level 10, equity 10, leverage 6, coordination 5, climate 4 — as a ranking, with mandate tier as a hard constraint, and allocates the same total dollars the status quo spends.

If the model is right, that scenario buys measurably more asset-life for identical money. That result, computable and reproducible, is the conclusion you want people to reach — and they reach it themselves, by clicking a preset, after having already played with the sliders and failed to beat it.

Keep the optimizer transparent and boring: greedy by marginal-TCO-avoided per dollar, mandate tier as a hard floor, and the full ranked project list on screen. A visible ranking anyone can audit beats a clever solver nobody trusts.

§8 — The frame

Logos and pathos, side by side, at the end

Placement matters more than content here. Put this at the end — after someone has played with the model. Leading with it reads as an attack; earning it reads as a conclusion.

Card A · logos

How the model reads it

Four columns, nothing else: claim → evidence → source → confidence.

  • Every number links to a source id, page, and date
  • Ranges where the source gives a range ($1.65–2.5B), never a false point estimate
  • A visible "known gaps" panel — you already maintain one
  • At least one falsifiable prediction, dated
  • Symmetric: the same scrutiny applied to arguments you agree with

Card B · pathos

How the ballot reads it

Code every sentence of all eight argument documents into one of seven categories, then count.

  • Verifiable claim · unverifiable claim · value appeal · fear appeal · identity appeal · attack on opponent · no content
  • Stacked bar per document, quotes pulled and tagged
  • Headline metric: % of text containing a checkable number — and how many check out
  • Then: what the arguments never mention. Debt service totals. Sunset dates. The consent decree. What happens if it fails.

Neither card tells you how to vote. One tells you what it costs. The other tells you how to feel about it.

The rule that makes this analysis instead of a hit piece: code all four documents per measure, both sides, identically. If the "in favor" and "against" arguments both come out 80% appeal and 20% checkable, that is a finding about ballot arguments as a genre — which is a far stronger and more durable point than scoring one side. Publish the coding rubric and the raw coded text so anyone can re-run it.

One judgment call worth making deliberately. Host this on BeTH, whose stated positioning is "neutral scoreboard — surfaces patterns, does not prescribe," and let d1-site link to it. A pathos-coding of live ballot measures published on a candidate's own domain gets read as campaign material and the method gets discounted; the same analysis on the transparency hub gets read as method. Same content, different half-life. Check the FPPC treatment either way before the measures are on the ballot.

§9 — The path through

What a first-time visitor does, in order

Eight steps, each one sentence of text and one thing to touch. No walls of prose anywhere in the product.

  • The clock. Twenty seconds. Shock, then a visible "why is this moving?" control.
  • The stack. Where it comes from — categories, sized against each other.
  • Whose fault is this? The mandate tiers. Most of it isn't discretionary. This deflates blame and raises the stakes at the same time.
  • Streets. A road they drive on. Concreteness after abstraction.
  • Could we have done better? Replay the deferral curve: what the FY2017 $524M backlog would have cost to fix then, against ~$1.8B now.
  • What can we do? The overlay, free play, no guidance.
  • What does the optimizer say? The same dollars, ranked. The punchline.
  • The two cards. Now that they've done the reasoning, show them the two ways of talking about it.

§11 — Risks

The five things that could sink it

  • Double counting. Your own budget-spine caveats already flag it: pension sits inside departmental personnel lines and Non-Departmental, and interfund transfers double-count against department totals. Decompose before aggregating, or the first CPA who looks discredits the whole thing.
  • The merged headline. Adding pension UAL to capital backlog to get one big scary number is the single most likely fatal error. Two tickers. Explicit, toggleable merge.
  • False precision on thin data. Facilities has no comprehensive condition assessment — the workbook says so. Render low-confidence classes visibly differently: wider bands, muted, flagged. Precision you don't have is the tell.
  • A number nobody can reproduce. Mitigated only by shipping the model alongside the page, with a golden-file test proving they agree.
  • Election-timing optics. Covered in §8 — host on BeTH, keep the coding symmetric, publish the rubric, check FPPC treatment early.
