# A Rational Budgeting Process for Berkeley

**Reform framework grounded in 54 audits, 384 recommendations, and the FY2027–FY2028 Proposed Budget · Berkeley Transparency Hub · 2026-06-10**

## What the data actually shows

Three facts, all from the City's own documents, define the problem:

1. **The deficit is recurring and is closed with one-time money every year** — $10M (2019) → $40M (2021) → $27M → $22M → $12M → $17M → $29M (2026). Every year was "balanced" with reserves, ARPA, fund balance, transfers, the Section 115 Trust, or vacancy savings. None resolved the structure.

2. **The baseline was padded.** The FY27–28 budget re-based to actuals and cut the General Fund personnel budget by **$16.5M / −7.4%** from baseline to proposed — Salaries −$9.45M, Pension −$3.68M, Health/Retiree −$1.58M, Overtime −$0.71M. A budget that can shed $16.5M by "re-baselining to actual spending" was, by definition, carrying $16.5M of appropriation that didn't reflect reality. That is the mechanism by which an adopted budget becomes fiction: it appropriates against padded baselines, not actual need.

3. **Personnel is the structural driver and is barely touched on the cost-sharing side.** Salaries & benefits are 66–67% of the General Fund. The largest lines (FY27 proposed) are Salaries $105.0M, Pension $54.6M, Health/Retiree $21.9M. The City's own narrative says the "majority of [benefit] increase [comes] from health and pension costs." The budget attacks this almost entirely through attrition (164 fewer positions) rather than through the recurring cost of benefits per remaining employee.

And the audit record shows the City has a standing, ignored playbook for the rest: of 384 recommendations, 76 were dropped (many by silent 5-year lapse) and 43 are open — concentrated in the highest-spending departments (Public Works 33% implemented; City Manager 50%), and concentrated in exactly the categories a structural fix needs (Efficiency & Effectiveness 47%, Transparency & Accountability 29%).

The conclusion is not "the numbers are fake." It is: **the process produces an adopted budget that poorly predicts actual spending, defers its own structural fixes, and leaves the largest recurring cost driver to attrition.** A rational process fixes the process, not just the year.

## Design principles

1. **Truth in budgeting** — the adopted budget should approximate what is actually spent. Minimize the adopted → amended → actual gap and publish it.
2. **Structural balance** — recurring revenue funds recurring expense; one-time money funds only one-time needs. No exceptions hidden in transfers.
3. **Outcome accountability** — every program states a goal and a measure; allocation follows results; audit findings feed the budget.
4. **Cost discipline on the real driver** — personnel cost is managed on both sides: workload-based positions *and* benefit cost-sharing, not attrition alone.
5. **Protected funds** — reserves and replacement/internal-service funds are rule-bound and cannot be silently raided.

## The process — eight mechanics, each tied to an audit revelation

### 1. Baseline integrity (zero-based to actuals)
Re-base every department to trailing actual expenditures annually; no automatic escalation of unspent lines. Vacant positions budgeted at true cost and removed after a fixed vacancy window unless re-justified.
*Audit basis:* the City just demonstrated this works — $16.5M came out of the GF personnel baseline. Institutionalize it instead of doing it once in a crisis. *Confidence: High.*

### 2. Carryover reform (appropriations lapse)
Operating appropriations lapse at fiscal year-end. Only **encumbered** funds (goods/services already under contract) and **separately-budgeted capital** carry forward, and only by explicit re-appropriation with written justification. Capital projects live in a multi-year capital fund, not the operating budget, so operating carryovers stop masking true operating cost.
*Audit basis:* the *Financial Condition* audit (2022) called for a capital/deferred-maintenance funding plan (dropped); commingling capital timing with operating budgets is part of why all-funds department totals swing wildly year to year (e.g., Public Works $187M → $234M → $213M).
*Data gap:* the size of Berkeley's current carryovers is not in the budget packet — it requires the year-end fund-balance schedules and CAFR. Flag for sourcing. *Confidence: Medium on magnitude, High on direction.*

### 3. AAO discipline (stop the mid-year drift)
Consolidate Annual Appropriations Ordinance amendments to a fixed quarterly cycle. Each amendment must (a) identify a specific funding source, (b) be net-neutral to fund balance, or (c) require a Council supermajority. Publish a standing **adopted → amended → actual** reconciliation so the public can see how far final spending drifts from what Council adopted.
*Audit basis:* the recurring "no performance measures / no documented procedures" theme (Zero Waste, Code Enforcement, Contracts audits) reflects a culture where money moves without a tracked rationale; AAO discipline forces the rationale onto the record.
*Data gap:* AAO amendment history/magnitude is not in this packet — it requires the AAO ordinances themselves. *Confidence: Medium on magnitude, High on direction.*

### 4. Personnel cost-sharing (manage the driver, not just the headcount)
Bargain employee contributions toward health-premium and pension cost across MOUs, phased in. The GF Health/Retiree line is $21.9M and Pension is $54.6M; these and salaries are the whole structural problem. Cutting 164 positions reduces *count* but leaves *per-employee benefit cost* — the actual growth driver, by the City's own statement — unaddressed.
*Audit basis:* *Employee Benefits: Tough Decisions Ahead* and the *Financial Condition* Section 115 Trust recommendation (dropped) both point here.
*Honest caveats:* (a) This is a **bargaining** outcome, not a unilateral lever — it requires labor negotiation and cannot be booked by fiat. (b) The Health/Retiree line bundles active-employee health with retiree OPEB; the active-vs-retiree split and current contribution rates need the MOUs and CalPERS schedules before any savings figure is credible. The lever is real (High); a specific dollar figure is **Low confidence** until that data is in hand. Do not publish a savings number without it.

### 5. Performance-based allocation
Every program submits a goal and a measurable outcome with its budget request. Programs that cannot state a measure, or that miss targets, are flagged for review before funding is renewed — not cut automatically, but no longer funded on autopilot.
*Audit basis:* this is the single most repeated audit finding — missing performance measures and outcome tracking in Zero Waste (2014/2016), Code Enforcement (2018), Library program evaluation (2020), Restaurant Inspections (2024), and Homeless Response Team (2025, all 6 recommendations still Not Started). The audits already wrote the requirement; the budget process should enforce it. *Confidence: High.*

### 6. Audit-accountability loop (end the silent 5-year lapse)
The budget adoption packet must report, per department, the status of every open and relevant audit recommendation. **Dropping a recommendation requires an explicit Council vote with a written cost-benefit** — ending the practice where a fix is "dropped" simply because five years passed.
*Audit basis:* 76 of 384 recommendations dropped, a large share by automatic lapse rather than decision; the *Financial Condition* deficit fixes themselves were dropped "to allow for updated recommendations" in a later report. Tie budget adoption to recommendation status and deferral becomes a recorded choice, not a default. *Confidence: High.*

### 7. Reserve and replacement-fund rules
Adopt a replenishment schedule for the Stability and Catastrophic reserves and protect internal-service/replacement funds: no diversion without a documented cost analysis showing the impact, approved by Council.
*Audit basis:* *Fleet Replacement Fund Short Millions* (2021) — 11 of 12 recommendations still only Partly Implemented while $2M (FY25) and $0.5M (FY24) were transferred out of the Equipment Replacement Fund; *General Fund Reserve Policy Fails to Convey that Maintaining the Reserve is a Priority*; the dropped reserve-replenishment recommendation. *Confidence: High.*

### 8. Revenue integrity
Maintain complete, current inventories of grants, leases, and fees; track receivables; set cost-recovery fees where allowed. Capture revenue the City is already owed before cutting services.
*Audit basis:* *Citywide Grants Management* (2016, all 15 dropped — grant revenue and clawback risk); *2009 Leases Audit Follow-Up* (2025, lease inventory still not centralized 16 years on); franchise-fee recovery and fee-study items in Zero Waste and Restaurant Inspections; the *Business License Taxes* audit (subtitled "…Will Increase Revenues"). *Confidence: High.*

## How the eight mechanics close the loop

| Symptom in the data | Mechanic that addresses it |
|---|---|
| $16.5M of padded personnel baseline | 1 Baseline integrity |
| Adopted budget ≠ actual spending | 2 Carryover + 3 AAO discipline |
| Personnel cost growth (health/pension) left to attrition | 4 Cost-sharing |
| Programs with no measurable goals (the top audit theme) | 5 Performance allocation |
| 76 recommendations dropped, many by lapse | 6 Audit-accountability loop |
| Replacement/reserve funds raided | 7 Fund rules |
| Grant/lease/fee revenue left uncaptured | 8 Revenue integrity |
| Recurring deficit closed with one-time money | Principle 2, enforced by 1–3 and 7 |

## Independent assessment (what I'd tell Council, unvarnished)

The strongest, hardest-to-rebut claim here is **not** that the deficit is fake. There is real cost growth — 8% expense vs. 6.5% revenue over FY17–26 — that no process change fully erases. The defensible claim is narrower and more damaging: **a large share of the gap is a governance choice.** The City can re-baseline $16.5M when forced to; it has a standing audit playbook for efficiency and revenue it mostly ignored in its biggest departments; and it has left the dominant cost driver — benefits per employee — to attrition rather than cost-sharing. Process reform plus benefit cost-sharing plus revenue capture would not eliminate the structural deficit, but the evidence supports the claim that they would materially shrink it and end the annual one-time-money ritual.

Two cautions, because they will be the rebuttals:
- **Carryover and AAO discipline have legitimate limits.** Encumbrances and multi-year capital genuinely must carry; the reform is transparency and control, not literal "no carryover."
- **Health cost-sharing is bargained, not decreed.** It belongs in MOU negotiation, and its savings can't be scored until the active/retiree health split and current contribution rates are sourced.

## Data still needed to quantify the case
