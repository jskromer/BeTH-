If Measures U and V fail: the remediation ledger

The City’s own Plan B for a failed sales tax is a $9.4 million cut list. The recurring gap is $29 million a year. This page sets the City’s list beside every remediation measure already in Berkeley’s audit record, sized where a source exists, with the labor-contract calendar that governs the one lever large enough to close the gap on its own.

Bottom line

A $9 million list for a $29 million problem.

If Measure V fails, the City has said it will close Fire Station 4, lay off police officers and cut recreation. That covers the one-time money the tax was meant to replace. It does not touch the structural gap, which the City has closed with one-time money for seven straight years. The measures that could, from re-basing to actuals to benefit cost-sharing, are all in the audit record, and most were dropped. Only one of them is big enough on its own, and it is bargained, not voted.

The pattern the Auditor named in 2022, and whose fixes the City dropped: a Section 115 funding plan, a reserve-replenishment plan, an enterprise-fund balance policy, and a capital funding plan. Four of five recommendations were “deferred to allow for updated recommendations.”

Where the money went instead. The General Fund under-spent its budget every year FY2020–24 and swept $30–42M a year out to capital, the pension trust and reserves ($42.0M in FY2024). Combined banked reserves: about $80M (Stability $30.6M, Catastrophic $23.7M, Section 115 $26.5M). The deficit is a forward projection, not a past cash shortage; the surplus was real and was walled off by policy. High See Two Fixable Problems.

Tier A and B measures are process fixes with mostly unverified yields; some produce cash, some avoid future cost. Their defensible claim is narrower than “this closes the deficit”: they are the relief valves that do not require cutting police, firefighters or a public vote, and most sit in the Auditor’s dropped pile. What has to be modeled before anyone quotes a number

  • Current employee contribution rates for health premiums and pension, by unit, and by classic vs. PEPRA membership. Source: the MOUs (public) and the Compensation Matrix (revised December 2025).
  • The split inside the $21.9M Health/Retiree line between active-employee health and retiree OPEB. Source: FY2025 ACFR OPEB note; budget detail.
  • CalPERS employer contribution rates by plan (Miscellaneous, Police, Fire) and the unfunded liability schedule. Source: CalPERS actuarial valuations (public).
  • Then: a scenario table, 1 / 2 / 3 percentage points of additional employee contribution, by unit, by year, against the contract calendar above. About one week of work once the MOUs are read.
The honest framing. Cost-sharing is bargained, cannot be booked by Council vote, and any figure before the model is a guess. The audit record is the basis for asking, not for a number. Published figures on this site will carry the model’s date and assumptions or will not be published.
05

The counterargument

Strongest case against this page

The City already cut 10.3% of the General Fund in ongoing reductions, re-based to actuals, and has told voters plainly what a failed tax costs. That is more fiscal candor than most cities manage. Half the General Fund is police and fire, so any further cut lands on public safety whatever the process reforms say. The Tier A and B measures are real but small and slow; the City’s own channel for the big lever is the bargaining table, and the fire contracts are at it now. A sales tax is the one revenue the Council can put to a simple-majority vote, and $9–10M a year of it buys time for the slower fixes to land. Refusing the tax does not make the audit recommendations implement themselves. Moderate

Our assessment: the candor is real, and so is the arithmetic. The sales tax closes a third of the recurring gap and leaves two-thirds to the same one-time methods as the last seven years. The remediation stack does not close it either, except through the bargained lever, and that is why the bargaining calendar is on this page. Whichever way the vote goes, the question for the first budget update after November 3 is the same: what is the City asking for at the fire table, and what will it ask for at the 2027 tables.

06

How this page will be used after November 3

  • If U and V pass: this page becomes a ledger of what the City said it would do with the money, tracked against the budget’s own list (the 33 preserved positions, Station 4, the bond’s project list and its $40.5M implementation set-aside).
  • If either fails: this page becomes the live remediation tracker. Each Council and Budget & Finance action (AAO amendments, station closure, layoffs, MOU items, audit-recommendation status) is logged weekly as a dated event against the stack in section 03, with its source.
  • Either way: the first entries are the fire-contract status, the AAO#1 amendment for FY27, and the Auditor’s FY2025 financial condition report, where the dropped 2022 structural fixes were to be re-issued.
