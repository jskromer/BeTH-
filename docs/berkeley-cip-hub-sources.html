The Infrastructure Dashboard Referral: Sources, Data & What Was Actually Built

On September 29, 2026 the Council adopted, on consent, a referral asking City staff to build a public dashboard tracking infrastructure projects, modeled on a UC Berkeley student prototype. This page lists every data source behind that prototype, every project loaded into it, and what the referral is buying.

AThe referral

Item 26, Consent Calendar, September 29, 2026. Author: Mayor Adena Ishii. Co-sponsors: Councilmembers Taplin, Tregub and O'Keefe. Title: "Referral to the City Manager to develop an updated Cross Departmental Program Plan and Strategic Asset Management Plan per the Realize Vision 2050 Plan to address the City's Long-term Infrastructure Needs and Maintenance Schedule and Provide Semi-annual Progress Reports to City Council." High

Action. The annotated agenda records "Approved recommendation." The consent calendar was adopted in one motion (Taplin/Tregub), all ayes. The Agenda & Rules Committee had moved the item from the Action Calendar to Consent between the draft and certified agendas. High

What it asks for. Three things. First, an updated cross-departmental Program Plan: "all the infrastructure projects Citywide that need to be completed, attached with a rough cost analysis of each project and a prioritization plan informed by urgency and the Realize Vision 2050 plan." Second, an updated Strategic Asset Management Plan to track maintenance across asset life cycles. Third, a "transparency hub dashboard to publicly track its infrastructure projects," which "could be akin to the Transportation Studio: Capital Improvement Program (CIP) Hub created by the UC Berkeley students," and which "will track current project schedules and their budgets, as well as integrate future capital planning." The City Manager is to report to Council every six months on both plans. High

Cost. "Approximately $200,000, which includes staff and consultant time," for the two plan updates. The referral gives no separate figure for the dashboard, its data layer, or ongoing maintenance. The $200,000 is the sponsor's estimate; staff have not costed it. High

The need figure. The referral says "approximately $1.5 billion." The Mayor's Task Force report of December 2, 2025 says "approximately $2 billion." The student report says about $1.65 billion. The City's bond page has used $1.65–2B and the FY2027–31 proposed CIP cites $2.1B. These are different definitions and years, not a trend. High that each is the published figure.

BWhat the students built

The referral's footnote 1 points to a Google Site. Behind the site is a report, a policy brief, and a live ArcGIS dashboard. Here is each one.

The report

Improving the CIP Prioritization and Tracking Process in Berkeley, February 2026. Authors Bim-Ray Yau, Samuel H. Arnett and Yutong Li, UC Berkeley Master of City Planning, CP 218 Transportation Planning Studio. Client: the Mayor's Office (Matt Nichols acknowledged). Professor Karen Trapenberg Frick acknowledged. High

Its findings on the City's current process: the FY2025–2029 CIP holds 118 projects totaling about $346 million in ten categories; several prioritization rubrics coexist (Budget Referral tiers, Public Works priority labels, pedestrian and bike plan scoring) with no public explanation of how they connect; the public GIS portal shows only existing assets; the permits web map has no cost, timeline or responsible party for any project. At about $15 million a year, the report projects network PCI falling from 55 to 53 between 2024 and 2028. High

Its recommendations come in two parts. Part One is a scoring system: consolidate the rubrics into one public explanation; screen projects into the CIP first, then rank them on eight Project Impact metrics plus EBMUD-style Urgency metrics; use ISI Envision as a supplement only; set the weights with the community, district by district. The report supplies metrics and deliberately no weights. Part Two is a tracker: an ArcGIS dashboard, because the City already runs ArcGIS, with a plain-language description, score and rank, budget and funding status, timeline, on-time and on-budget flags, and a staff contact for each project. The model is Ann Arbor, which tracks 400+ projects with one staff member, updates annually, and exports from its planning software (Allovance) into the map layer. High

Its own stated limits: weights not set; "every CIP project still needs a data record"; map symbology unresolved; citywide projects have no map representation; staff time and upkeep not costed. One section cites "200+ CIP projects" against the 118 cited earlier, unreconciled. High

The dashboard

The ArcGIS item is titled "CP218 Test Template," with the on-screen title "Project Tracker Template — Berkeley CIP, FY 25-29," and the item snippet "Test Sample for City of Berkeley Transportation Studio." It was created November 4, 2025 and last modified December 15, 2025. Its data layers were populated December 16, 2025; read-only "view" copies were added May 12, 2026. Everything is public under a UC Berkeley ArcGIS account. High

It has one map, three project lists (points, lines, polygons), three category filters, a legend, and an "About" panel. The lists show three fields: project name, "On Time?" and "On Budget?". The category filter runs on proj_cat. No other field is surfaced in a widget. High

The underlying layers carry ten attribute fields: proj_title, proj_cat, cip_score, onbudget, ontime, budgetsize, timeline, description, contact, plus the system ID. Budget and timeline are stored as text strings copied from the CIP PDF ("$ 2,656,245", "FY2024-FY2026"), so they cannot be summed or sorted without cleanup. The on-time and on-budget values are hand-entered "Yes," "No" or "N/A" with no date, no source and no stated rule for how they were decided. High

EWhat the referral is buying

The gap

The referral says the student hub "will track current project schedules and their budgets, as well as integrate future capital planning." The students' own item is titled "Test Template," holds 16 of 118 projects with values copied by hand from a PDF, surfaces two status flags with no date or rule behind them, has no score, no contact, no funding source and no capital-planning data loaded, and the report says the full data layer still has to be built. The $200,000 covers the two plan updates. Nothing in the referral funds the data layer, the schema, or the staff time Ann Arbor's experience says a tracker needs. High on each fact; the conclusion is ours.

What a usable version needs, drawn from the report and the Ann Arbor interview: a record for every CIP project; budget and schedule stored as numbers and dates, with the adopted value and the current value kept separately so "on time" and "on budget" are computed and dated; the funding source; the adopted target the project serves (PCI, sidewalk backlog, facility condition); a staff owner; a last-updated date; a machine-readable export; and a named person whose job includes updating it.

The counterargument

A proof of concept is supposed to be thin. The referral asks staff to build the real thing, and a City-run dashboard with an accountable staff owner is more durable than any outside tracker. Ann Arbor shows one person can maintain 400+ projects once the setup is done. The student report is also the first public document to say plainly that Berkeley's scoring rubrics are unexplained and its project data is not public, which is more than the City had said about itself. The referral's six-month reporting cycle is a real commitment, and the first report is due around late March 2027. Moderate on the due date (six months from September 29, 2026; no date is set in the referral).

What to watch: whether the first semi-annual report arrives on time, whether it carries any project-level data, and whether the Program Plan update the Task Force set for Spring 2026 and the AMP steering committee it set for July 2026 appear in it. As of this writing neither has surfaced in the public record. Low (absence of evidence).
