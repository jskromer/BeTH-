Berkeley Budget Accountability: FY2016-FY2026

A year-by-year view of budget responsibility, infrastructure underinvestment, and the operating costs that crowded out asset maintenance.

Core finding

Berkeley did not simply “lose” the maintenance money. Over the last decade, growing resources were absorbed into personnel, pensions and healthcare, overtime, public liability, homeless and housing programs, public safety, council initiatives, and one-time budget patches while infrastructure needs accumulated.

Blunt conclusion: Responsibility is collective, but not equal. Long-serving budget actors and city management had repeated opportunities to shift money toward asset maintenance. Instead, the city mostly protected services, staffing, labor costs, housing/homelessness priorities, and new council initiatives while deferring the infrastructure bill.

Where the money went instead

The strongest evidence comes from the City Auditor’s financial-condition reporting for FY2016-FY2025. The city’s spending pressures were not mysterious: personnel, pensions, healthcare, overtime, insurance, and one-time fiscal patches consumed fiscal capacity that could otherwise have been directed toward asset maintenance.

General Fund pressure points

  • Salaries and wages.
  • Pension and retirement costs.
  • Healthcare benefits.
  • Overtime.
  • Public liability insurance.
  • Housing, homelessness, public safety, and council priority initiatives.
  • One-time budget patches instead of structural correction.

Examples of one-time fixes included redirecting money intended for workers’ compensation, drawing from the pension Section 115 Trust, and relying on federal ARPA funds to balance earlier budgets.

Accountability framing

A precise criticism is not merely that Berkeley “overspent.” The sharper fiscal-reality argument is:

Berkeley systematically consumed infrastructure capital faster than it replenished it.

Roads, parks, public buildings, storm drains, marina facilities, information systems, and other public assets aged while operating expenditures and service commitments grew. The responsibility sits with successive councils, mayors, city management, and the political incentives created by voters and advocacy groups. But those who served across multiple budget cycles had the clearest repeated opportunities to change course.
