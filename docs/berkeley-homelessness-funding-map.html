The people

A night's count, as 100 people

86 single adults · 7 in families with children · 6 unaccompanied youth (households, HMIS 2025)

60+ unsheltered · 49 chronically homeless (a year+ plus a disabling condition)

52 Black or African American — from 10% of the county's population · 30 aged 55+ · ~10 aged 65+

The funnel these hundred stand in: 25,000+ people touch the county system in a year → 8,201 counted on one January night (−13% since 2024) → 975 in Berkeley's region (flat since 2024, while the county fell) → 320 beds inside Berkeley, 56 of them city-referred.

The cost ladder (derived from Home Together 2030's own modeling — annual, per household or unit):

prevent ≈ $7K · shelter ≈ $21K · PSH ≈ $32K · resolve one encampment ≈ $4.7–5.4M · CareBridge ≈ $267K/room over 3 yrs

Every step rightward on the ladder is roughly 3–4× the one before it — and the county funds ~500 prevention households a year against a modeled need of 4,700.

Marcus, 58 — long-term unsheltered homelessness

Marcus illustrates what navigating services can look like for an older adult who has been unsheltered for years and has a disabling condition.

  1. Lives at an encampment. Counted every other January.
  2. HRT outreach reaches him — but HRT can refer directly to only 56 beds, and paused case conferencing for a year.
  3. If his site gets an ERF grant (three have): a motel room the city controls. That door cost $4.7–5.4M per site.
  4. If not: the CES queue — 280 days average, and fewer than a quarter of PSH referrals housed within a year.

His PSH year, once housed: ≈$32K. His years outside: crisis, ER, and enforcement costs no Berkeley document totals.

Elena, 34, two kids — the invisible 7%

  1. Evicted after a rent increase. Prevention assistance would have cost ≈$7K — the county funds ~500 such households a year; the modeled need is 4,700.
  2. Doubles up with relatives — invisible to the PIT count, avoids shelter over child-welfare fears. Families are 7% of the counted system but undercounted by design.
  3. Reaches rapid rehousing through the community-agency pot HSPE scores (~$4.8M) — a time-limited subsidy (717 slots countywide today; the plan wants 1,400).

Her whole path runs through the one funding stream whose fund goes negative in FY2026.

Ray, 47 — the crisis path

  1. Psychotic episode on the street. Someone calls.
  2. Before FY27: the city's Mobile Crisis Team — already down to 2.6 filled positions, 5 days a week. Eliminated.
  3. Now: BPD (part-time CIT coordinator), or the county's mobile crisis teams — whose duty comes from the DRC settlement, which never says what's owed inside Berkeley. The endpoints the DOJ flagged: John George or Santa Rita.

Ray is why the DRC settlement's silence on city jurisdictions, and Home Together 2030's silence on the settlement, are the same gap.

Dee, 19 — the 6% youth

  1. Aged out of foster care, couch-surfing — mostly uncounted, exactly the pattern research ties to adult homelessness later.
  2. Berkeley's BHSA early-intervention solicitation included a transition-age youth contract estimated at $100K/yr, one of five planned contracts. Homelessness is one of the adverse outcomes the program is intended to reduce.

The November 17 budget scan lists the subsequent contract amounts.

Walter, 68 — the fastest-growing case

  1. Fixed income, rent outpaces it. One in ten of the counted are now 65+; the plan calls older-adult homelessness "increasingly urgent."
  2. Sleeps in his car — counted, but low priority scores.
  3. Waits in the queue for a PSH unit that can handle aging needs — the scarcest kind: the plan models a need for 2,000 medically-frail PSH units against 50 today.

Preventing Walter: ≈$7K once. Housing him after: ≈$32K every year, in a unit type that barely exists.

The 26 of 28 — a reported outcome

Berkeley's BHSA plan reports that 26 of 28 people from unsheltered settings secured permanent, long-term housing through a city behavioral-health program.

  1. Unsheltered, engaged by a city-funded team.
  2. Behavioral-health services plus a housing pathway, together.
  3. Permanent housing — 93%, measured and reported.

The green path shows the outcome the city reported for this group.
