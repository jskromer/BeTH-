Zero Waste: rates up, still losing

The refuse enterprise. A five-year rate increase adopted in 2023 was supposed to keep the fund positive while its balance fell from $24.5M to $3.3M. Two years in, it has lost $4.0M and $1.3M and sits near zero.

Bottom line

The rate plan's cushion is already gone.

Council raised refuse, recycling and organics rates in June 2023 for FY2024 through FY2028, on a staff projection that the fund's balance would decline but stay positive. The audited result: an operating loss of $5.4M in FY2024 and $1.5M in FY2025, net position $349,593 at the start of FY2025. The 2014 and 2016 audits' efficiency recommendations, 18 of 27 of them, were dropped, including one sized at $496,000 a year.

01

How the fund was set up

The Zero Waste Fund collects refuse, recycling and organics rates from residents and businesses and pays for collection, the transfer station, street sweeping and illegal-dumping cleanup. Under Proposition 218 rates can rise only after a noticed hearing without a majority protest. The current schedule is Resolution 70,932 (June 27, 2023). High

What the ledger shows

Finding 1

Two losing years after a rate increase.

Operating loss $5.4M (FY2024) and $1.5M (FY2025). Net position $769,103 at June 30, 2024, restated to $349,593, and the fund is heading for the General Fund's door the way the Marina did. High

Finding 2

The efficiency list was dropped, not rejected.

18 of 27 recommendations from the Zero Waste audits lapsed: a route audit to align billing with service, franchise-fee recovery on recyclables, mobile route technology, a strategic plan with performance measures, and biweekly collection (about $496K a year, needing a state waiver). High

Finding 3

The next costs are capital.

The transfer station replacement and an electric fleet (about $15.5M for vehicles and chargers, staff estimate) are not in the 2023 rate schedule's assumptions. Moderate

09

The counterargument

Strongest case against this page

The 2023 rate plan was designed to spend down a $24.5M balance; losses in its first years are the plan working, not failing, and FY2024's loss was inflated by one-time costs. Proposition 218 limits how fast rates can move. Dropped audit recommendations include some that needed state waivers the City cannot grant itself. The fund's problem is cost growth in a labor-heavy service, which no efficiency list fully offsets. Moderate
